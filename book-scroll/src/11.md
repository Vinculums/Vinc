# What Are Nodes and Edges

Chapter 10 split memory into six Node kinds: Concept, Episode, Record, Decision, Bridge, Entity. This chapter looks at what that split actually does, and why an Edge — the relationship that joins two Nodes — is never just a line.

## A Node Is a Contract, Not a Label

Writing "Concept" on a Node is different from that Node actually being treated as a Concept. If a Node is a Concept, it means the Node gets referenced repeatedly, becomes a retrieval candidate, and can form relationships with other Concepts. An Episode, by contrast, means an event recalled in chronological order. Retrieval can name an Episode too, but what it names is a moment rather than a meaning, and the system handles the two differently once they are named.

So a Node's kind isn't decoration. It's closer to a contract that decides in advance how that Node will be handled inside the system. Label something a Concept and treat it like an Episode, or the reverse, and retrieval and relationship traversal start to drift apart.

## Why Six Kinds

Each kind answers a different question.

- Concept: what does this mean (a persistent idea)
- Episode: what happened, and when (an event on a timeline)
- Record: what was observed or confirmed (supporting evidence)
- Decision: what was chosen (the outcome of a judgment)
- Bridge: where do separate domains meet (a connection point)
- Entity: what is this called (not yet a formal concept, but still needs identification)

Five of the six are closed: the system decides what a Concept, an Episode, a Record, a Decision or a Bridge is, and handles each in its own way. Entity is the open one. Whatever else a person authors, under whatever type name they choose, lands there, and the name they chose enters their vocabulary as a proposal for them to ratify. Chapter 13 returns to that vocabulary.

Take a single sentence: "we decided to adopt a new vector database." At least three kinds of information are tangled together in it — the concept of that database itself (Concept), the choice to adopt it (Decision), and the circumstances at the time that choice was made (Episode). Store all three as one undifferentiated blob of text, and later on, a question like "why did we pick this technology back then" becomes hard to answer. Split them by kind, and you can go straight to the piece the question actually needs.

## An Edge Carries Direction and Meaning Together

Knowing that two Nodes are connected isn't enough information by itself. If A and B are connected, whether A caused B, whether A is a precondition B needs first, or whether they're just loosely related — these are entirely different claims.

That's why an Edge has both a name and a direction.

```text
A ──prerequisite_of──> B
```

This notation doesn't just say A and B are linked. It makes a specific claim: A has to be established before B can be. Flip the direction and write the same relation from B to A, and the meaning changes completely.

VINC deliberately keeps the vocabulary of relations small: relates_to (loose association), prerequisite_of (precondition), derives_from (derivation), inherits_from (inheritance), about (what something concerns). The vocabulary stays narrow on purpose — every new relation name adds another thing everyone has to agree on again: what exactly does this relation mean.

## Allowed Pairings, and Pairings That Aren't

Not every Node kind can connect to every other kind through any relation you like. A relates_to between two Concepts feels natural. The same relates_to between two Decisions probably needs to mean something different — Decisions are usually chained by lineage (one decision superseding another), not by vague association.

Without this constraint, a graph can look dense on the surface while saying nothing underneath. A graph where everything connects to everything through relates_to is functionally no different from a graph that draws no distinctions at all.

## Why Split Node and Edge

To sum up: a Node decides what exists. An Edge decides in what capacity those things meet each other. Keeping the two separate is what lets you later ask much sharper questions of the graph than "everything related to this concept" — questions like "what had to exist before this concept" or "what was derived from this concept."

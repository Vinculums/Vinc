# development economics

## One-line essence
*Why are some countries poor and some rich* — a discipline that has changed its answer five times over 80 years, with the fourth answer (institutions) and the fifth answer (RCTs) currently underway.

## Intuitive picture
In 1950 India's per capita GDP was 200 dollars, Korea's 70 dollars. In 2020 India's was 2,000 dollars, Korea's 32,000 dollars. From the same starting point, a *160-fold gap* arose. *Why?* — development economics is the discipline of this one question.

The five scholarly answers:
1. **Capital shortage** (1950s Lewis, Rosenstein-Rodan, Rostow): capital accumulation is the essence. The *Big Push* + the *Take-off*. → India's five-year plans, the Soviet industrialization model.
2. **Dependency** (1960s–70s Frank, Cardoso, Wallerstein): poverty is not *the Third World's problem* but *a world-system problem*. The core sucks up the periphery's resources. → Latin American ISI, the *de-dependency* movement.
3. **Market liberalization** (1980s Washington Consensus): government failure is greater. Free trade + privatization + free capital. → IMF and World Bank SAPs, applied to Latin America and Africa, the *lost decade*.
4. **Institutions** (from the 1990s, Acemoglu, North): neither capital nor market but *institutions* are the essence. *Inclusive vs. extractive* institutions. → *Why Nations Fail* (2012).
5. **RCT micro policy** (from the 2000s, Banerjee, Duflo): *small experiments* instead of grand theory. Mosquito nets, textbooks, microfinance. → *Poor Economics* (2011), the 2019 Nobel.

These five answers are not *mutually contradictory* but answers at *different time scales*. Capital (short-run), dependency (systemic), market (institutional tool), institutions (long-run), RCT (policy tool). Studying development economics means *holding the five answers simultaneously* + *judging the fit of time and space scales*.

- **The East Asian miracle (1965–1995)**: Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore + 1980s China + 2000s Vietnam. All five answers worked *partially* — capital (savings rates 30%+) + market (export orientation) + institutions (a strong state + land reform) + the dependency critique (protection then liberalization). *Integration is the essence*.

- **The African divergence (from the 2000s)**: 1990s Afro-pessimism → a 2000s takeoff (BRI, mobile money, the resource boom). But capital shortage + weak institutions + climate shocks simultaneously — a case where *the five answers operate at once*.

- **The Indian model**: 1947 socialism (Nehru) → 1991 liberalization → 2020s digital public infrastructure (UPI, Aadhaar). A *non-East-Asian, non-Chinese* third way.

## Mathematical structure

**Solow-Swan growth (1956)** — capital + labor + technology:
$$Y = K^\alpha (AL)^{1-\alpha}, \quad \dot{K} = sY - \delta K$$
- The steady-state per capita capital $k^* = (s/(\delta+n+g))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$
- The absolute-convergence hypothesis: all countries converge to the same steady state — a *predictive failure* (the gap widened)

**Conditional convergence**: $k^*$ *differs by country* (savings rates, population, technology-absorption rates) → the *convergence club* hypothesis (Quah 1996)

**The Lewis model** (1954): unlimited labor supply (rural) + the capitalist sector → industrialization. Rural wage = subsistence level, industrial wage = subsistence + α. *High growth* until the surplus labor is absorbed.

**Diff-in-Diff (RCT causal inference)**:
$$\hat{\tau} = (Y_T^{post} - Y_T^{pre}) - (Y_C^{post} - Y_C^{pre})$$
- The treatment group's change minus the control group's change. The causal estimation of the *policy effect*.

**Measuring institutions** (Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson 2001 *AER*):
- Colonial settler mortality → the formation of *extractive institutions* → per capita GDP differences 200 years later.
- Instrumental-variable regression: $Y_i = \alpha + \beta \text{Inst}_i + \epsilon, \quad \text{IV} = \text{settler mortality}$

**The Doing Business indicators** (World Bank 2003–2021): business registration, contract enforcement, taxes, and so on — the *quantification of institutions* — discontinued in 2021 (a manipulation exposure) — the earnest recognition of the *methodological limits* of quantitative development economics.

## Why it matters

Development economics is *the ethical testing ground of economics*. It confronts head-on the *non-Western, colonial, and poor* that the eight giants (06) externalized. The sharpest form of the question *for whom is the discipline*.

Deeper positioning:
- **The integrated testing ground of the four theoretical disciplines (01–04) + political economy (06)**: The micro (prices, markets), the macro (growth, money), behavioral (poverty traps, self-control), games (aid dynamics), and political economy (institutions, colonialism) are all *verified/falsified in development cases*
- **Central to real policy**: The SDGs, the MDGs, climate adaptation, UBI, digital public infrastructure, basic education, women's empowerment — the scholarly foundation of every global policy
- **The limits against real markets**:
  - *Western-scholar-centeredness* (apart from Sen, Banerjee, Lin)
  - *Measurement defects* (PPP, purchasing power, the informal sector, women's unpaid labor)
  - *The multiple-equilibria problem* (the Big Push hypothesis is a *coordination failure* — simple causal inference is difficult)
  - *The externalization of climate* (growth models ignore natural resources and climate)
- **The emerging development scholarship**:
  - **Acemoglu-Robinson** *Why Nations Fail* (2012) — the essence of institutions + the 2024 Nobel
  - **Lin Justin Yifu** *New Structural Economics* (2012) — derived from the Chinese experience
  - **Chang Ha-Joon** *Kicking Away the Ladder* (2002) — the advanced countries *kick away the ladder after their own development*
  - **Mariana Mazzucato** the *entrepreneurial state* — development = mission policy
  - **Hickel** *Less is More* (2020) — degrowth development

Where development economics collapses (*the changing definition of development in the age of climate, AI, and population aging*, *the absence of non-Western scholars*), the next discipline springs up.

## Historical context (the R-03 economic-variant four acts + integration of the five external-variable axes)

### Act 1 — The emergence of development economics (1945–1970)

**Colonial liberation** (India 1947, Indonesia 1949, Ghana 1957, 17 African countries in 1960). *Development* became a *scholarly agenda* for the first time. **The Marshall Plan** (1948) — the *large-scale capital-transfer model* of European reconstruction.

**Lewis** (1954, 1979 Nobel): unlimited rural labor + industrialization. **Rosenstein-Rodan** (1943) the *Big Push* — simultaneous multi-sector investment is required. **Hirschman** (1958) *unbalanced growth* — stage-by-stage entry. **Rostow** (1960) *The Stages of Economic Growth* — a five-stage evolution (traditional → preconditions for take-off → take-off → maturity → mass consumption).

**Structuralism (Latin America)**: the **Prebisch-Singer hypothesis** (1950) — the long-run decline of primary-commodity prices. **ECLAC** (1948) — recommending *import-substitution industrialization (ISI)*. The standard Latin American policy of the 1950s–70s.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 1)**:
> - **Global currents**: The end of WWII, colonial liberation, the Cold War, the founding of the UN
> - **Great figures**: Lewis, Rosenstein-Rodan, Rostow, Hirschman, Prebisch, Mao, Nehru, Nasser
> - **Technological revolution**: The Green Revolution (Norman Borlaug 1960s) — the *physical foundation* of development economics
> - **National cultures**: India (socialist planning), China (Mao), Latin America (ISI), Japan (postwar revival), Africa (single-party developmental states)
> - **Social change**: The population explosion (1950–75, 2.5 billion → 4 billion), urbanization, the transfer of colonial power

**Decisive transition**: The late-1960s limits of ISI (Latin American inflation, foreign-exchange shortages) + the export-oriented success of East Asia (Taiwan, Korea from 1965) → the entryway to the next act's *development-paradigm branching*.

### Act 2 — Dependency theory + the East Asian miracle (1970–1990)

**Dependency theory**: **Frank** (*Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America*, 1967) — not *low development* but *under-development* (active impoverishment). **Cardoso** (*Dependency and Development*, 1969 — later president of Brazil). **Wallerstein** (*The Modern World-System*, 1974) — the *world system* of core, semi-periphery, and periphery.

**The East Asian miracle**: Korea 1965–95 grew 8.4% a year, Taiwan 8.8%, Hong Kong 7%, Singapore 8%, Japan 4.5%. **Amsden** (*Asia's Next Giant*, 1989 analysis of Korea), **Wade** (*Governing the Market*, 1990 Taiwan), **Johnson** (*MITI and the Japanese Miracle*, 1982). The formalization of the *developmental state* model.

**The Lewis-Furtado-Cardoso Nobel** (1979) — the *scholarly recognition* of development economics.

**The foreign-exchange crises of the 1980s (South America, Africa)**: the 1973 and 1979 oil shocks + the 1980 Volcker interest rates → the 1982 Mexican default → the *lost decade* (Latin America 1980–89). **IMF and World Bank SAPs** (Structural Adjustment Programs) — liberalization, privatization, austerity. The political defeat of dependency theory.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 2)**:
> - **Global currents**: The oil shocks, the Latin American debt crisis, China's 1978 reform and opening, Soviet stagnation
> - **Great figures**: Frank, Cardoso, Wallerstein (dependency), Amsden, Wade, Johnson (East Asia), Sen in earnest (development ethics), Deng Xiaoping (China)
> - **Technological revolution**: The container (the explosion of global supply chains), semiconductors, telecommunications (the takeoff tools of Korea and Taiwan)
> - **National cultures**: The East Asian developmental states (Korea, Taiwan, Japan, Singapore), the limits of Latin American ISI, the limits of African single-party rule, China's *socialist market*
> - **Social change**: The formation of the East Asian middle class, the Latin American urban poor, African urbanization, the 1980s explosion of U.S. NGOs

**Decisive transition**: The East Asian success + the Latin American failure → the scholarly branching over *which policy is right*. The confrontation of SAP critics (Sen, Stiglitz) vs. defenders (Williamson's Washington Consensus) in earnest.

### Act 3 — The Washington Consensus + critique and the emergence of RCTs (1990–2008)

**The Washington Consensus** (1989 Williamson): fiscal discipline, tax reform, interest-rate liberalization, exchange rates, trade liberalization, foreign investment, privatization, deregulation, property rights — 10 items. The **IMF and World Bank standard** + applied to Latin America, Africa, and the Eastern bloc.

**Critique**:
- **Stiglitz** (*Globalization and Its Discontents*, 2002) — ignoring information asymmetry
- **Easterly** (*The Elusive Quest for Growth*, 2001) — the *empirical failure* of IMF and World Bank policy
- **Rodrik** (*Has Globalization Gone Too Far?*, 1997) — a critique of the universal prescription
- **Chang Ha-Joon** (*Kicking Away the Ladder*, 2002) — the advanced countries *kick away the ladder after their own development*

**Sen's capability** (1999 *Development as Freedom*) → **HDI** (UNDP 1990) → **MDG** (Millennium Development Goals, 2000–2015) — *non-GDP indicators* became the policy standard.

**Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson** (2001 *AER*) — colonial mortality → institutions → GDP 200 years later. The quantitative verification of the proposition that *institutions are the essence of development*.

**The RCT revolution**:
- **Banerjee-Duflo** **J-PAL** (MIT, founded 2003) — randomized controlled trials of poverty policy
- **Kremer**'s Kenyan school RCTs (from the 1990s) — the quantitative effects of textbooks and deworming
- **Chattopadhyay-Duflo** (2004 Indian women's political quotas)
- Poverty = *cognitive, information, and trust limits* (the integration of behavioral economics + development)

**The rise of the China model**: 1978–2012, 9.5% annual growth. WTO accession (2001) → overtaking Japan in 2010 (GDP no. 2). An *East Asia + socialism + market* mixture.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 3)**:
> - **Global currents**: The Soviet collapse, the WTO, the rise of China, 9/11, the African takeoff (from the 2000s)
> - **Great figures**: Williamson, Stiglitz, Easterly, Rodrik, Chang, Sen, Acemoglu, Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, Lin Yifu, Modi, Lula
> - **Technological revolution**: The internet, the mobile phone (African mobile money, M-Pesa 2007), biotechnology (HIV drugs, deworming), financial engineering
> - **National cultures**: China (market socialism) + India (1991 liberalization), the Latin American *Pink Tide* (Lula), the African takeoff (Mobile), East Asia and the IMF (after the 1997 crisis)
> - **Social change**: The reduction of global poverty (1990 36% → 2015 10%), the explosion of the Chinese middle class, the rise of globalization's losers (the rust belt)

**Decisive transition**: The 2008 financial crisis → the *final political death* of the Washington Consensus. At the same time, the rise of the **China model** (a 4-trillion-yuan stimulus, recovering faster than the West). The entryway to the next act's *West vs. China development-model competition*.

### Act 4 — Multipolarity, digital, climate, China (2008–present)

**The China model in earnest**: 1980 per capita 200 dollars → 2020 10,000 dollars. **BRI** (the Belt and Road Initiative, from 2013) — an 8-trillion-dollar infrastructure pledge to developing countries. *State capitalism + market + technology leapfrogging*.

**Lin Yifu** *New Structural Economics* (2012) — turning the Chinese experience into scholarship. *Following comparative advantage + government industrial policy*.

**India's digital public infrastructure**: **Aadhaar** (biometric ID, 1.4 billion), **UPI** (digital payments from 2016, 10 billion transactions a month), **Direct Benefit Transfer** — the *digital public infrastructure (DPI) model* of development.

**The African takeoff**: 2000–14 growth of 5%+ a year (the resource boom + mobile and Chinese investment). **M-Pesa** (Kenya 2007) — the global model of mobile money. But slowing from 2014 with falling resource prices, COVID, and climate shocks.

**The Banerjee-Duflo-Kremer 2019 Nobel** — the apex of RCT development economics.

**The Acemoglu-Robinson 2024 Nobel** — the apex of institutional development. **Acemoglu** *Power and Progress* (2023) — AI determines *the distribution of power*.

**Climate development economics**: The 17 SDGs (2015–30) — integrating poverty + climate + equality. **Climate finance**, the **Loss and damage fund** (COP27 2022). **The green transition** (Indian solar, African electrification) — *development + climate integration* in earnest.

**Degrowth**: **Hickel** *Less is More* (2020), **Latouche** — a critique of development itself. The alternative of *shrinking the rich countries + developing the poor*. Expanding academic + political influence (2020s Europe).

**Mazzucato** *Mission Economy* (2021) — mission-oriented development. Influencing EU, UK, and UAE policy.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 4)**:
> - **Global currents**: Multipolarity, the rise of China, BRI, COVID, the Russia-Ukraine war, climate, AI
> - **Great figures**: Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, Acemoglu, Robinson, Lin Yifu, Mazzucato, Hickel, Modi, Xi, Bukele, Bola Tinubu (Nigeria)
> - **Technological revolution**: AI, mobile, blockchain, digital public infrastructure, climate technology, the autonomous economy (smart cities)
> - **National cultures**: China (BRI), India (DPI), Africa (M-Pesa, resources, climate), Latin America (the leftward return), Islam (UAE Vision 2030, Saudi NEOM), Southeast Asia (digital + development)
> - **Social change**: The stagnation of global poverty (COVID pushed 100 million back into poverty), climate justice, the youth population (Africa's average age is 19), digital labor, the AI job threat

**Decisive transition** (present): AI + climate + multipolarity → *the definition of development itself is changing*. Is *GDP growth = development*, or is *climate, equality, and capability = development*? After the five answers, *a sixth answer is forming* — the domain where this note enters academic tracking mode.

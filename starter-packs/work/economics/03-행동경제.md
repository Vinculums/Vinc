# behavioral economics

## One-line essence
Humans are not rational utility-maximizers but *systematically* biased decision-makers, and this bias has *predictable patterns*.

## Intuitive picture
Are you rational? Compare the two gambles below.

- **A**: 1 million won with 100% probability
- **B**: 2 million won with 90% probability, 0 won with 10% probability (expected value 1.8 million won)

Most people choose A. Even though its expected value is *lower*. *Certainty* is nice. This time, let us flip the sign.

- **C**: a *loss* of 1 million won with 100% probability
- **D**: a *loss* of 2 million won with 90% probability, 0 won with 10% probability (expected value −1.8 million won)

Most people choose D. To *avoid* a loss, they accept risk. The *same* person is risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses. The utility-maximization model cannot explain this *asymmetry* — the decisive crack in the rationality assumption was exposed in a single 1979 Kahneman-Tversky experiment.

This asymmetry is the starting point of behavioral economics. Beyond it:
- **Anchoring**: After the question "Is the proportion of African countries among UN members higher or lower than 65%?" one is asked for an estimate — even though one does not know the actual answer, *65%* influences the estimate. *The brain takes an irrelevant number as its anchor*.
- **Availability**: Right after seeing an airplane accident, one *overestimates* the risk of flying. *Recent and vivid* information distorts probability judgment.
- **The endowment effect**: The price demanded when selling a mug one has been given is *twice* the price one would pay before buying it. The same object is more valuable *when it is in one's hand*.
- **Status-quo bias**: The enrollment rate in organ-donation *opt-in* countries is ~15%, in *opt-out* countries ~95%. The *default option* governs the decision.
- **Hyperbolic discounting**: "100 won today vs. 110 won in one week" → 100 won today. "100 won in one year vs. 110 won in one year and one week" → 110 won in one year and one week. *The discount rate changes with temporal distance* — a violation of the standard exponential-discounting model.

All of these patterns are not *accidental mistakes* but *systematic biases*. The same person in the same situation is irrational *repeatedly* in the same way. This *predictability* is the foundation of the possibility of a discipline — the essence of behavioral economics is that irrationality too can become a *science*.

## Mathematical structure

**Prospect Theory (Kahneman-Tversky 1979)** — the combination of two functions:

The value function $v(x)$ — measured *relative to a reference point* instead of utility:
$$v(x) = \begin{cases} x^\alpha & \text{if } x \geq 0 \\ -\lambda(-x)^\beta & \text{if } x < 0 \end{cases}$$
- $\alpha, \beta \approx 0.88$ (the curvature of the concave and convex parts)
- $\lambda \approx 2.25$ — the **loss-aversion coefficient**: a loss of the same size weighs *2.25 times more* than a gain
- The reference point is the *current state* (not absolute wealth)

The probability-weighting function $\pi(p)$:
$$\pi(p) = \frac{p^\gamma}{(p^\gamma + (1-p)^\gamma)^{1/\gamma}}, \quad \gamma \approx 0.61$$
- Small probabilities are *overweighted*, large probabilities *underweighted* — explaining the simultaneous purchase of lottery tickets and insurance

The expected value (prospect value):
$$V = \sum_i \pi(p_i) \cdot v(x_i)$$

→ *Mathematically incompatible* with Expected Utility Theory. After its 1979 appearance it eroded standard decision theory over 30 years.

**Hyperbolic Discounting (Laibson 1997 quasi-hyperbolic)**:
$$U = u_0 + \beta \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \delta^t u_t \quad (\beta \approx 0.7, \delta \approx 0.99)$$
- $\beta$ — the *present-bias* coefficient. A future-vs.-future comparison uses standard discounting, a today-vs.-future comparison uses an *additional discount $\beta$*.
- The quantitative explanation of procrastination, undersaving, and diet failure.

**Anchoring** (Tversky-Kahneman 1974):
$$\hat{x} = a + \alpha(x - a), \quad 0 < \alpha < 1$$
- The estimate $\hat{x}$ is *partially pulled* toward the anchor $a$. If $\alpha < 1$, a *complete correction fails*.

**Default Effect** (Madrian-Shea 2001):
- With 401(k) automatic enrollment, the enrollment rate is ~85%, with opt-in ~50%. A 35-percentage-point gap from the *difference in default option* of the same action alone.

**Mental Accounting (Thaler 1985)**:
$$U = \sum_k u_k(W_k), \quad \sum_k W_k = W$$
- Wealth $W$ is separated into *mental accounts* $W_k$. The standard model is a single $U(W)$ — the behavioral model is the *summation of separated utilities*. *Non-substitutability* between different accounts (salary vs. bonus vs. gambling winnings).

## Why it matters

Behavioral economics is the discipline that *integrated economics with psychology and neuroscience*. The key is that it presented not a mere critique but an *alternative model* — which is why it is being absorbed into standard economics.

Deeper positioning:
- **The systematic exposure of microeconomic assumptions**: Utility maximization, rational expectations, time-consistent preferences — all empirical violations. But because the violations are *systematic*, an *alternative model is possible*. The *the model is a map of the domains where the model breaks* pattern of the micro (note 01) is applied in earnest
- **Integration with the macro**: Akerlof-Shiller (*Animal Spirits*, 2009), Shiller (*Irrational Exuberance*) — behavioral factors in macro volatility, asset bubbles, and financial crises. Acknowledged by Bernanke (Fed minutes).
- **Being turned into a policy tool**: The nudge as a *policy of minimal freedom infringement* — libertarian paternalism. Nudge policy units were standardized at the UK BIT, the OECD, the World Bank, and others.
- **Neuroeconomic and computational-economic integration**: Direct measurement of the *neural mechanism of prospect theory* by fMRI (Tom, Fox, Trepel, Poldrack 2007). LLMs *reproduce human biases* (Binz-Schulz 2023) — an input to artificial-intelligence alignment and interpretability.
- **Real policy effects**:
  - U.S. 401(k) automatic enrollment: savings rate +30%
  - UK organ-donation opt-out conversion: enrollment rate +10%
  - U.S. power-display change: energy saving
  - Korea's Behavioural Insights Team (from 2018): tax-payment and pension-enrollment nudges

Where behavioral economics collapses (*the origin of the human biases LLMs show in AI-vs.-human comparison*, *the limits of cross-cultural generalization*), the next discipline springs up — 4 self-identifications in this note.

## Historical context (the R-03 economic-variant four acts + integration of the five external-variable axes)

### Act 1 — Emergence of theory (1955–1979)

**Herbert Simon** (*Administrative Behavior*, 1947 + *Models of Man*, 1957) — *bounded rationality*. Humans are not rational utility-maximizers but *satisficers* who seek a *good enough* outcome. 1978 Nobel. But *isolated* within mainstream economics.

**The emergence of cognitive psychology** (1956 Miller "Magical Number 7", 1960s Newell-Simon information-processing model). The cognitive revolution after behaviorism (Skinner) formed the *foundation for reconnecting psychology to economics*.

**Tversky and Kahneman** (from an Israeli-army psychology background, collaborating from 1969) — 1974 *Science* "Judgment under Uncertainty" — the first formalization of the three great heuristics and biases (representativeness, availability, anchoring). A psychology paper *fell like a shell onto economics*.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The Cold War, the cognitive revolution (from 1956), the fusion of psychology and computer science. After the Vietnam War, *trust in rational decision-making models* weakened.
> - **Great figures**: Simon (a multidisciplinary giant), Tversky and Kahneman (from Israeli-army psychology → heroes of the cognitive revolution). The theory sprang up *outside* mainstream economics — the *external-ignition* pattern of scholarly change.
> - **Technological revolution**: The cognitive-psychology experimental paradigm, statistical-inference tools, computer simulation. The *possibility of formal analysis* of behavioral data emerged.
> - **National cultures**: Israel (Tversky and Kahneman's army-psychology background — the *national-security environment* was the soil of the discipline), the United States (Simon's multidisciplinary environment at Carnegie Mellon), Britain (Edwards's decision theory). **The decisive contribution of the Israeli school** is a case of the discipline's *geopolitical multiplicity*.
> - **Social change**: The 1960s civil-rights and antiwar movements → skepticism toward *rational authority*. Psychological criticism of decision-making was *culturally accepted*.

**Decisive transition**: The 1974 Tversky-Kahneman *Science* paper — the path was opened to *quantitatively criticize economic rationality* with psychological tools. The formal theory exploded in the 1979 prospect theory.

### Act 2 — Formalization (1979–2000)

**Prospect Theory (1979 *Econometrica*)**: Kahneman-Tversky published in the *top economics journal*. The four core concepts of the value function, probability weighting, loss aversion, and the reference point. An *alternative formal model* to Expected Utility Theory. Thirty years later, one of the most-cited *Econometrica* papers.

**Cumulative Prospect Theory (1992)**: Tversky-Kahneman remedied the mathematical defect of the first model (the violation of probability weighting). The *apex of prospect theory* + acquisition of standard-tool status.

**Thaler (University of Chicago)** — began behavioral application *from within economics*. Mental accounting (1985), the endowment effect (1980), a continuous "anomalies" column (1987–1991 *JEP*). *Behavioral vocabulary penetrated* mainstream economics.

**Loewenstein and Prelec** — time preference, regret theory. **Camerer** — behavioral application of game theory (level-k thinking). **Rabin** (1998) — a behavioral-economics synthesis *JEL* review.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The height of neoliberalism (the 1980s) — rational expectations, monetarism, and trust in the market were the scholarly standard. *Behavioral economics was an anti-mainstream movement*.
> - **Great figures**: Kahneman (2002 Nobel, Tversky died in 1996), Thaler (a Chicago *renegade*, 2017 Nobel), Loewenstein, Camerer, Rabin, Laibson. **The behavioral faction within the Chicago school** was decisive — behavioral economics grew in the home base of Friedman and Lucas.
> - **Technological revolution**: Experimental economics (Vernon Smith 2002 Nobel), the emergence of neuroeconomic tools (1990s fMRI). An explosion of the possibility of the *micro dimension* of data analysis.
> - **National cultures**: The United States (Chicago, Berkeley, MIT, CMU), Britain (the LSE behavioral group), Israel (the heirs of Tversky and Kahneman). Behavioral economics is *a network of scholars rather than a school* — a distributed scholarly format.
> - **Social change**: The introduction of the 401(k) (1981, United States), the popularization of the stock market, the explosion of consumer credit. The *increasing economic weight* of individual decision-making → behavioral patterns rose as a policy concern.

**Decisive transition**: The 2002 Kahneman Nobel → behavioral economics was recognized as *part of mainstream economics*. But *policy application* was the domain of the next act in earnest.

### Act 3 — Market verification and policy application (2000–2010)

**Madrian-Shea (2001 QJE)**: 401(k) automatic enrollment → a 35-percentage-point rise in the enrollment rate. The first large-scale case of the *quantitative verification of behavioral policy*. U.S. 401(k) automatic enrollment became the standard.

**Sunstein-Thaler 2003 *JLS***: "Libertarian Paternalism" — minimizing freedom infringement + *inducing* better decisions. It offered a political-justification tool.

**Thaler-Sunstein 2008 *Nudge***: A general-public bestseller. It established the concept of *choice architecture*. The U.S. Obama administration and the UK Cameron administration adopted it as *national policy*. **The UK Behavioural Insights Team** (from 2010) — the world's first government behavioral-policy unit.

**Ariely 2008 *Predictably Irrational***: Disseminated behavioral economics to general readers. Generalized additional biases such as *asymmetric dominance, the decoy effect, and the zero-price effect*.

**Camerer (2003) Behavioral Game Theory**: Integrated behavior into game theory — quantal response, level-k, k-level reasoning. Combined game theory + experiment.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The 2000 dot-com bubble + the 2008 financial crisis — *rational expectations* collapsed in the face of *systematic mass mania*. Akerlof-Shiller's *Animal Spirits* (2009) applied behavior to the macro crisis.
> - **Great figures**: Sunstein (Obama's OIRA administrator), Thaler (policy adviser), Ariely (popular dissemination), Cass Sunstein and Cass Halpern (UK BIT). **A direct connection from scholar to policymaker**.
> - **Technological revolution**: An explosion of A/B tests, internet data, and field experiments. The *possibility of large-scale verification* of nudge effects.
> - **National cultures**: Britain (the BIT, a world model), the United States (Sunstein's OIRA), Australia, Singapore, the UAE, Korea (a behavioural insights team in 2018) — *national behavioral-policy units* were standardized. **The turning of state power into a behavioral-economics tool**.
> - **Social change**: The digital environment (app and web nudges), climate-change awareness, the retirement-savings crisis (the imminent retirement of the baby boomers). The social demand for behavioral tools surged.

**Decisive transition**: The 2008 financial crisis was a decisive exposure of *rational expectations* → behavioral economics rose as a *scholarly-paradigm candidate*. But an *alternative standard model* was incomplete — the integration task of the next act.

### Act 4 — Reinterpretation and integration (2010–present)

**Neuroeconomic integration**: **Glimcher** (*Foundations of Neuroeconomic Analysis*, 2011) — direct measurement of the neural circuits of value determination (vmPFC, striatum). **Tom-Fox-Trepel-Poldrack (2007 *Science*)**: the neural mechanism of loss aversion — value representation itself is asymmetric. Behavioral economics secured a *biological foundation*.

**RCT and development-economics integration**: **Banerjee-Duflo** (J-PAL, founded 2003) — RCT verification of poverty policy. **2019 Nobel**. Behavioral economics + development economics = the standard of *evidence-based policy*.

**Mullainathan and Bertrand** — behavior + poverty (Bertrand's 2004 discriminatory-résumé audit study), behavior + machine learning (Mullainathan-Spiess 2017). Combined with **Athey and Imbens**'s machine-learning causal inference.

**The LLM and AI era** (from 2020):
- **Binz-Schulz 2023 *PNAS***: GPT-3 *reproduces* human cognitive biases — anchoring, availability, the certainty effect, and so on. The possibility of AI as a *human-cognition simulator*.
- **Horton 2023**: The possibility of LLMs as *substitute subjects* in behavioral experiments. A steep drop in the cost of experimental economics.
- **AI alignment**: Behavioral-economics findings are a *direct input to AI alignment* — the risk that learning human values *also learns the biases*.

**Climate behavioral economics** (from 2020): Carbon neutrality, climate nudges, climate *loss framing* (protection vs. gain). COVID mask and vaccine nudges — behavioral economics as a *public-health tool*.

**Recognition of limits** (from 2010):
- **The limit of cultural generalization**: Behavioral biases are biased toward *Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic (WEIRD)* samples (Henrich-Heine-Norenzayan 2010). Non-Western verification is lacking.
- **The shrinking of effect sizes**: In meta-analysis, prospect-theory effect sizes shrink 50% from the original papers (the Open Science Collaboration 2015 replication crisis).
- **Nudge effect size**: An average effect size of ~1.4 percentage points (Mertens et al. 2022 meta-analysis) — small for policy + publication bias.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The standardization of behavioral policy after 2008, COVID (nudges, vaccination), the climate crisis (carbon nudges), the Russia-Ukraine war, the advent of AI. **Every great event is a new application domain for behavioral tools**.
> - **Great figures**: Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer (2019 Nobel), Mullainathan, Glimcher, Camerer, Sunstein. **The age of development, neural, and AI integration**.
> - **Technological revolution**: The generalization of fMRI, RCTs, machine learning, LLMs, digital nudge platforms. *Behavioral data* as one domain of big data.
> - **National cultures**: The United States, Britain, India (development + behavior), Korea (a behavioural insights team in 2018), China (the broad application of CCP digital nudges). The rise of the ethical gray zone of the **combination of authoritarianism + behavioral economics**.
> - **Social change**: Libertarian paternalism vs. the critique of freedom infringement, digital manipulation, AI ethics, climate justice, intergenerational distribution. The *political-philosophical critique* of behavioral economics in earnest.

**Decisive transition**: The 2010s replication crisis and the recognition of the limits of cultural generalization → behavioral economics entered a *stage of humility*. It is proceeding to a new stage along the four axes of *large samples, cross-cultural work, a neural foundation, and LLM integration*.

### Core insight

The four acts of behavioral economics are the 60 years from the *systematic exposure of the rationality assumption* to the *formalization of an alternative model*. A discipline that *grew outside* the utility-maximization assumption of the micro (01) transformed the discipline itself *as it was absorbed into the mainstream*. But *the shrinking of effect sizes, the limits of cultural generalization, and the change in the definition of behavior in the LLM era* are the new limit points of this discipline. Behavioral economics is a *question about the future of economics itself* — if the utility-maximization model is *not fundamentally wrong but partially wrong*, then how to integrate the two models is unsolved. The same insight as the school-multiplicity pattern of the macro (02) — *the end of the era of the sole hegemony of a model*.

## Self-identification of new unsolved problems (U-14 format, economics N=3)

1. **The limit of WEIRD generalization**: Behavioral biases are Western-sample-centered — verification of whether the same patterns hold in the non-Western world and developing countries is lacking (Henrich 2010 onward). The discipline's *cultural universality* is unsolved
2. **The replication crisis**: The core prospect-theory experiments shrink 50% in effect size in meta-analysis. *The authenticity of the original effects* is unsolved
3. **Publication bias in nudge effect size**: Mertens et al. 2022 meta-analysis — an average effect of 1.4 percentage points, small for policy. *The actual value of behavioral policy* is unsolved
4. **The origin of LLM behavioral biases**: GPT-3 reproduces human biases — is it a bias of the training data, the essence of statistical inference, or the universal structure of human cognition? *The essence of behavior* itself is unsolved
5. **The political philosophy of libertarian paternalism**: The nudge is justified as minimal freedom infringement, but where is the boundary between *manipulation vs. information provision*? In the age of digital nudges and AI manipulation, *the definition of a consent-based society* itself is unsolved

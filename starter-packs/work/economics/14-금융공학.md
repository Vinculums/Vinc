# Financial Engineering

## One-line essence
The price of a derivative comes from a single *no-arbitrage principle* — discount future cash flows under the *risk-neutral measure*, and Black-Scholes-Merton (1973) coded this simultaneously as a *partial differential equation* and a *stochastic integral*.

## Intuitive picture
If 01 microeconomics is *utility and equilibrium*, this 14 is the apex of applying that micro logic to *market prices of an uncertain future*. The core intuition is a single one — "there is no free lunch."

- **No-Arbitrage principle**: a portfolio that produces a certain profit with no risk cannot exist. If it exists, it is immediately traded away and vanishes. This *single axiom* uniquely determines derivative prices.
- **Replication**: an option's payoff can be *replicated* by a dynamic combination of *the underlying asset + a riskless bond*. The cost of replication = the option price. This is the heart of BSM.
- **Risk-Neutral Pricing**: if no-arbitrage holds, take the expectation not under the *real probability* but under the *risk-neutral measure* $\mathbb{Q}$ and discount at the riskless rate, and the price appears. The investor's risk preference is *cancelled out of the price* — a striking result.
- **Hedging**: whoever sells an option *holds the underlying in an amount equal to delta* to make the instantaneous risk 0. As the market moves, delta changes, so it must be *continuously readjusted* (dynamic hedging).
- **Greeks**: the sensitivities of the price — delta (underlying)·gamma (change in delta)·vega (volatility)·theta (time)·rho (interest rate). The trader's *everyday language*.

This elegant structure hinges on *one assumption* — that the underlying follows *geometric Brownian motion* and volatility is *constant*. Reality betrays that assumption with fat tails·jumps·the volatility smile, and the 2008 CDO collapse billed the price of that betrayal.

## Mathematical structure

**No-arbitrage = existence of a risk-neutral measure (the fundamental theorem of asset pricing)**:
$$\text{No arbitrage} \iff \exists\, \mathbb{Q} \sim \mathbb{P} \text{ s.t. the discounted price is a } \mathbb{Q}\text{-martingale}$$
- Harrison-Kreps (1979)·Harrison-Pliska (1981) formalization — the existence of an *equivalent martingale measure* $\mathbb{Q}$ is equivalent to no-arbitrage. Uniqueness ⟺ market completeness.

**Black-Scholes partial differential equation** (Black-Scholes 1973·Merton 1973):
$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0$$
- $V(S,t)$: option price·$S$: underlying·$r$: riskless rate·$\sigma$: volatility
- Derivation ① (PDE): the option + underlying delta-hedge portfolio is *riskless* → its return must be $r$ → the PDE

**Stochastic derivation (risk-neutral expectation)**: the underlying is geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure
$$dS_t = r S_t\, dt + \sigma S_t\, dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}$$
$$V_0 = e^{-rT}\, \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\text{payoff}(S_T)]$$
- Derivation ② (stochastic): the Feynman-Kac theorem connects *PDE ⟺ expectation*. The two derivations give the same answer.

**European call option closed-form solution**:
$$C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \quad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r \pm \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$
- $N(\cdot)$: standard-normal cumulative distribution·$K$: strike·$T$: maturity

**Greeks**:
$$\Delta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S},\quad \Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2},\quad \mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma},\quad \Theta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial t},\quad \rho = \frac{\partial C}{\partial r}$$
- delta hedge = neutralizing $\Delta$·gamma = re-hedge frequency·vega = volatility exposure

**Interest-rate models**:
$$\text{Vasicek (1977):}\quad dr_t = a(b - r_t)\,dt + \sigma\, dW_t \quad (\text{mean-reverting · Gaussian})$$
- CIR (1985) prevents negative rates via $\sigma\sqrt{r_t}$. Vasicek·CIR = *short-rate models*.
$$\text{HJM (1992):}\quad df(t,T) = \mu(t,T)\,dt + \sigma(t,T)\,dW_t, \quad \mu = \sigma \int_t^T \sigma(t,u)\,du$$
- Heath-Jarrow-Morton models the *entire forward-rate curve* — the no-arbitrage condition *forces* the drift into the volatility.

**Credit derivatives (Gaussian copula)** (Li 2000):
$$\tau_i = F_i^{-1}(\Phi(X_i)),\quad (X_1,\dots,X_n) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$$
- couples the *default correlation* across assets via a Gaussian copula. It became the industry standard for CDO tranche pricing — and the mathematical epicenter of the 2008 collapse.

## Why it matters

Financial engineering is the point where *01 microeconomics' utility·equilibrium* and *Math 06 probability·the Itô integral* meet to create a *trillion-scale industry*. The no-arbitrage principle extends microeconomics' equilibrium concept to *asset markets under uncertainty*.

Deeper position:
- **An extension of 01 microeconomics**: utility maximization → portfolio choice under risk (Markowitz) → no-arbitrage pricing. *Equilibrium* refined into *the cost of replication*.
- **Integration with 09 crisis·catastrophe**: violations of the BSM assumptions (fat tails·correlation spikes) are the seeds of a *systemic crisis*. LTCM (1998)·2008 CDO are clinical cases of model failure.
- **Integration with 13 neuroeconomics**: a trader's risk decision = NAcc reward vs dlPFC control neural competition (Bossaerts·Knutson). The neural absence of the *rational hedger* the model assumes.
- **The core of real-world policy**:
  - derivatives regulation (Dodd-Frank 2010·EMIR): central clearing·margin rules
  - Basel III capital regulation: VaR·CVaR-based risk capital
  - stress tests: supervision of model risk
- **Limits vs. the real market**:
  - *fat tails*: actual returns have thicker extremes than the normal distribution (Mandelbrot·Taleb)
  - *the volatility smile*: BSM assumes constant $\sigma$, yet the market's implied volatility *bends* by strike·maturity — addressed by local·stochastic volatility models (Heston·SABR·rough volatility)
  - *liquidity·transaction costs*: the continuous-hedging assumption is in reality discrete·cost-laden
  - *model risk*: the only tool used while knowing its assumptions are wrong

## Historical context (R-03 economics-variant 4 acts + external-variable 5 axes integrated)

### Act 1 — Prehistory: Brown·Bachelier (1900~1965)

**Bachelier** *Théorie de la spéculation* (1900) — modeled stock prices with Brownian motion, 5 years ahead of Einstein (1905) yet ignored by academia. **Itô** (1944) formalizes the stochastic integral (Math 06). **Markowitz** (1952) mean-variance portfolios — quantifying risk as *variance*. **Sharpe** (1964) CAPM — the price of systematic risk.

> **External-variable 5 axes (Act 1)**:
> - **World currents**: postwar reconstruction of capital markets·the Bretton Woods system
> - **Great figures**: Bachelier·Itô·Markowitz·Sharpe·Samuelson
> - **Technological revolution**: measure-theoretic probability (Kolmogorov 1933)·Itô calculus·early computers
> - **National culture**: France (Bachelier)·Japan (Itô)·the US (RAND·MIT)
> - **Social change**: the start of the popularization of individual investing·pension-fund growth

**Decisive turn**: Samuelson's rediscovery of Bachelier (1960s) + the introduction of geometric Brownian motion → the direct entrance to BSM.

### Act 2 — Formalization: the BSM explosion (1965~1985)

**Black-Scholes** *The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities* (JPE 1973) + **Merton** *Theory of Rational Option Pricing* (Bell Journal 1973) — a closed-form option price via no-arbitrage replication. The same year, **CBOE** opened (1973) — theory and market *born simultaneously*. **Harrison-Kreps** (1979)·**Harrison-Pliska** (1981) — the risk-neutral measure = the *martingale formalization* of no-arbitrage. **Cox-Ross-Rubinstein** (1979) the binomial tree — an intuitive discrete model.

> **External-variable 5 axes (Act 2)**:
> - **World currents**: the collapse of Bretton Woods (1971)·floating exchange rates·oil shocks → an explosion in demand for risk management
> - **Great figures**: Black·Scholes·Merton·Harrison·Kreps·Pliska·Cox·Ross·Rubinstein
> - **Technological revolution**: electronic calculators·CBOE electronic trading·standardization of Itô calculus
> - **National culture**: the US (MIT·Chicago·Chicago Board) as the overwhelming center
> - **Social change**: the institutionalization of derivatives markets·the birth of the quant profession

**Decisive turn**: 1987 Black Monday — the *first crack* in the BSM constant-volatility assumption. The volatility smile is imprinted on the market. The entrance to the next act's stochastic-volatility models.

### Act 3 — Extension·interest rates·credit (1985~2007)

**Vasicek** (1977)·**CIR** (Cox-Ingersoll-Ross 1985) short-rate models. **HJM** (Heath-Jarrow-Morton 1992) the entire forward-rate curve. **Heston** (1993) stochastic volatility — addressing the smile. **Merton** (1974) the structural credit model → **Li** (2000) the Gaussian copula CDO. **VaR** (JP Morgan RiskMetrics 1994) the risk standard. **LTCM** collapse (1998) — a hedge fund involving Nobel laureates (Scholes·Merton) went bankrupt from *model failure*.

> **External-variable 5 axes (Act 3)**:
> - **World currents**: globalization·the Asian crisis (1997)·the dot-com bubble·low interest rates
> - **Great figures**: Vasicek·Heath·Jarrow·Morton·Heston·Li·Merton·Scholes (LTCM)
> - **Technological revolution**: Monte Carlo simulation·PDE numerical methods·continuous-time finance
> - **National culture**: the US·the UK (the City of London) as the two great financial hubs
> - **Social change**: the securitization explosion·the spread of subprime

**Decisive turn**: 2007~08 subprime → the CDO Gaussian copula's *underestimation of default correlation* was amplified into a systemic crisis. Model risk turned into *macro catastrophe*.

### Act 4 — Post-crisis reconstruction + machine learning (2008~present)

**The 2008 lesson**: the Gaussian copula's *correlation = constant* assumption collapsed in the crisis (correlation spiked to 1). **Dodd-Frank** (2010)·**Basel III** — central clearing·capital regulation. **Rough volatility** (Gatheral-Jaisson-Rosenbaum 2018) — volatility as a *rough (H<1/2) fractal* path. **Machine-learning finance** — deep hedging (Buehler 2019)·neural-network option pricing. **Crypto-asset derivatives** (2017~)·**MEV** — the blockchain version of no-arbitrage.

> **External-variable 5 axes (Act 4)**:
> - **World currents**: the financial crisis·quantitative easing·COVID·low rates→inflation·AI
> - **Great figures**: Gatheral·Rosenbaum·Buehler·Cont·Avellaneda
> - **Technological revolution**: GPUs·deep learning·high-frequency trading·blockchain smart contracts
> - **National culture**: the US·UK·EU + Asia (Hong Kong·Singapore)·decentralized DeFi
> - **Social change**: strengthened quant regulation·model-risk supervision·the popularization of retail derivatives

**Decisive turn** (ongoing): rough volatility + deep hedging + DeFi = *financial engineering 3.0*. Replacing BSM's constant assumption with *data-driven paths*.

### Core insight

The essence of the 4 acts of financial engineering is the simultaneous progress of *the elegance of the single axiom of no-arbitrage* and *the repeated betrayal of the probabilistic assumptions beneath it*. BSM (1973) is *the most precise closed-form solution* economics ever produced, and a tool clinically exposed at the limits of its assumptions at each crisis (1987·1998·2008). **The model cannot predict the market, but it gives the market a *common language*** — delta·vega·the smile are now the market's reality. This is a feature of complex-systems scholarship — *the model changes its object (performativity, MacKenzie)*.

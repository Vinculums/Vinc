# economic history

## One-line essence
The 260 years of capitalism are an *oscillation between market and politics* — when the market runs wild, crisis brings political intervention, and when politics ossifies, back to the market again — and that oscillation has repeated four times.

## Intuitive picture
Economic *theory* is a model, but economic *history* is a flow of events. The same capitalist system manifested in *different forms each*: the British Industrial Revolution, Bismarck's German welfare state, the American New Deal, the Japanese developmental state, the Chinese socialist market, Islamic finance, and African mobile money. Theory assumes one model, but history shows *multiple capitalisms*.

- **The four market-politics oscillation cycles**:
  1. Free-market runaway (1760–1929) → the Great Depression → *political intervention* (the Keynesian consensus)
  2. Keynesian political ossification (1945–73) → stagflation → *return to the market* (neoliberalism)
  3. Neoliberal runaway (1980–2008) → the financial crisis → *political re-intervention* (quantitative easing, protectionism)
  4. COVID, AI, climate (2020–) → ?

  Theory asks "which side is right," but history answers "both sides are needed in turn."

- **Multiple capitalisms**: Britain (liberal), Germany (social market), Japan (developmental state), the United States (shareholder capital), China (state capital), Northern Europe (social democratic), Islam (sharia finance) — the same market mechanism works differently according to *national culture*.

- **Crisis is learning**: The Great Depression → Keynes, 1973 → rational expectations, 2008 → behavioral and financial macro. The discipline transforms with each crisis. The discipline's four acts (02 macro) are *synchronized with history's four acts*.

- **The outside of capitalism**: Colonies, slaves, women's unpaid labor, nature — all began as capitalism's *outside* and were absorbed into its *inside*. Four absorption cycles — feminist economics, ecological economics, and decolonial economics all grew out of this history.

## Mathematical structure

Economic history is a *narrative discipline*, but quantitative tools are also central:

**Maddison long-run GDP** — the 2000-year GDP time series converted to 1990 PPP$:
$$GDP_{world}(1) \approx 100B, \quad GDP_{world}(1820) \approx 700B, \quad GDP_{world}(2020) \approx 100T$$
- 1820–2020 = a 1000-fold increase. Population 7-fold + per capita ~140-fold.

**The Kuznets inverted U**: Inequality rises in early industrialization → falls later. Kuznets 1955. But Piketty (2014) criticized the *later-fall hypothesis as a temporary exception within capital dynamics*.

**Piketty's $r > g$** (return on capital > growth rate → the concentration of wealth proceeds automatically):
$$\beta = \frac{K}{Y} = \frac{s}{g}, \quad \alpha = r \cdot \beta$$
- The capital ratio $\beta$ is *savings rate / growth rate*. In both the 19th and 21st centuries $\beta \approx 6$; the mid-20th century was the *exception* (wars, taxes, unions).

**Reinhart-Rogoff (2009 *This Time is Different*)**:
- 800 years of financial-crisis data. Post-crisis recovery averages 6–8 years, unemployment rises 7 percentage points, housing falls 35%, government debt rises 86%.
- The debt/GDP 90% threshold controversy (the Herndon-Ash-Pollin 2013 exposure of data errors) — the political importance of quantitative economic history.

**Schularick-Taylor (2012)**: 140 years of credit data across 14 countries. *Credit explosion* is the strongest advance signal of crisis. Credit matters more than the money supply.

## Why it matters

Economic history is *the testing ground of theory*. Every school *verifies its own model with historical cases*. Keynesianism is verified/falsified by the Great Depression and the golden age, monetarism by stagflation and Volcker, neoliberalism by the 1990s golden age and 2008. The *one set of data, five schools* pattern (02 macro) is at its sharpest in economic history.

Deeper positioning:
- **The integrated verification ground of the four theoretical disciplines (01–04)**: Micro equilibrium, macro schools, behavioral biases, and game strategies all meet in *the multiple interpretation of historical events*
- **A real policy tool**: The 2008 response (Bernanke himself wrote his doctoral dissertation on the 1930s Great Depression), COVID (the 2020 large-scale fiscal response = learning from the 1930s New Deal), AI policy (learning from the labor shock of the First Industrial Revolution) — *history is the primary input of policy*
- **The limits against real markets**:
  - *Data defects* (omission of pre-1820, colonies, women's unpaid labor)
  - *Interpretive branching* (the cause of the Great Depression = monetary contraction (Friedman) vs. effective demand (Keynes) vs. financial frictions (Bernanke) vs. debt deflation (Fisher) — 4 schools, 4 interpretations)
  - *Presentism bias* (Western-centered, nation-unit analysis — the externalization of the colonial, women, and ecology)
  - *Unpredictability* (historical causation admits only *ex-post explanation*)
- **The input of emerging disciplines**: Feminist economics, ecological economics, decolonial economics, and historical capitalism (Wallerstein's world-systems) all grew out of *the limits of mainstream economic history*

Where economic history collapses (*the externalization of the non-Western world, women, and ecology*, *the absence of an ex-ante model for new events like COVID and AI*), the next discipline springs up.

## Historical context (the R-03 economic-variant four acts + integration of the five external-variable axes = *four acts = the four oscillation cycles*)

The four acts of this note are *the four market-politics oscillation cycles of economic history* — the most powerful self-consistent case of the R-03 variant.

### Act 1 — Free-market runaway → the Great Depression (1760–1929)

**The Industrial Revolution** (1760–1840 Britain, 1850–1900 Germany, U.S., Japan): textiles → steam → steel → electricity, chemicals, automobiles. The first appearance of the *factory system + urbanization + wage labor* structure. **Classical capitalism** (1820–1913) — the *first globalization* of free trade, the gold standard, immigration, and free capital movement.

**World War I** (1914–18): the end of 19th-century liberalism — the war economy, the introduction of controls, the temporary suspension of the gold standard, the Russian Revolution (1917). **The 1920s bubble** (U.S. stocks and real estate) → **the 1929 Great Depression**: world GDP fell 25%, unemployment 25%, the gold standard collapsed, protectionism exploded (Smoot-Hawley 1930).

**Fascism, the New Deal, the USSR** — three alternatives attempted simultaneously after the collapse of liberalism. The *self-adjustment hypothesis* of the free market was socially and politically rejected.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 1)**:
> - **Global currents**: British hegemony (Pax Britannica) → World Wars I and II → the collapse of liberalism
> - **Great figures**: Smith (theory), Marx (critique), Rockefeller, Carnegie, Rothschild (industrial and financial giants), Lenin, Roosevelt, Hitler (political responses)
> - **Technological revolution**: The steam engine, electricity, the internal-combustion engine, radio, the conveyor belt (Ford 1913). The *Second Industrial Revolution* was the *physical foundation* of the market's runaway
> - **National cultures**: British free trade, German protection + Bismarck's welfare (1880s), U.S. antitrust (1890s), Japan's Meiji (from 1868) developmental state
> - **Social change**: The formation of the working class + labor unions + universal suffrage + first-wave feminism + the beginning of colonial liberation movements

**Decisive transition**: The 1929 Great Depression was the decisive falsification of the free market's *self-adjustment hypothesis*. The legitimacy of *political intervention* was established. The entryway to the next act's Keynesian consensus.

### Act 2 — The Keynesian consensus and golden age → stagflation (1929–1973)

**The New Deal** (from 1933): social security, financial regulation (Glass-Steagall 1933), labor rights (the Wagner Act 1935). **World War II** (1939–45): the military-industrial complex, women's labor, the total-war economy. **Bretton Woods** (1944): the dollar-gold fixed exchange rate, the IMF, the World Bank. **The Marshall Plan** (1948): European reconstruction + American hegemony.

**The golden age** (1947–1973): The United States, Western Europe, and Japan had *annual growth of 4–6%*, full employment, and moderate inflation. The *grand compromise* of Keynesian policy + strong unions + progressive taxation + the welfare state. **The Glorious Thirty** (Trente Glorieuses) — the most equal 30 years in the history of capitalism.

**Japan and East Asia**: 1955–73 Japan grew 9% a year → the first verification of the *developmental-state model* (Korea and Taiwan followed from the 1960s). **The Soviet bloc**: the planned economy + Sputnik (1957) → the peak of *systemic competition*.

**The 1968 crisis signals**: student movements, antiwar movements, civil-rights movements → *cracks in the established cultural consensus*. The 1971 Nixon shock (the end of gold convertibility). **The 1973 oil shock** → inflation + unemployment *simultaneously* (stagflation) → the predictive failure of the Keynesian model.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 2)**:
> - **Global currents**: WWII + the Cold War (1947–91) + the Non-Aligned Movement + the Vietnam War + the oil shock
> - **Great figures**: Roosevelt, Keynes, Truman, Marshall, de Gaulle, Adenauer, Mao, Ikeda Hayato (income doubling), Park Chung-hee (the Korean developmental state), Kissinger
> - **Technological revolution**: Nuclear power, semiconductors (the 1947 transistor), space development, television, aircraft. **The advent of the computer** (mainframes in the 1950s, minis in the 1960s)
> - **National cultures**: U.S. Keynes + the military-industrial complex, the Western European social market, the Japanese developmental state, Soviet planning, Korean and Taiwanese authoritarian development, Indian socialism (Nehru), Islamic socialism (Nasser)
> - **Social change**: The spread of the middle class, women's labor (the war + 1960s second-wave feminism), colonial liberation (India 1947, Africa 1960s), the civil-rights movement (U.S. 1964)

**Decisive transition**: The 1973 oil shock + stagflation → the exposure of the Keynesian model's limits. The legitimacy of the *return to the market* formed. The entryway to the next act's neoliberalism.

### Act 3 — Neoliberal globalization → the financial crisis (1973–2008)

**Reagan-Thatcher** (from 1980): tax cuts, deregulation, union-busting, privatization, financial liberalization. **Volcker** (Fed chairman 1979–87) suppressed inflation with 20% interest rates — but the 1980 Mexican and Latin American debt crisis exploded.

**The collapse of the USSR** (1989–91) → *the end of history* (Fukuyama 1992) → the hypothesis of the free market's *worldwide universality*. **The WTO** (1995), **China's WTO accession** (2001) → the second globalization + the rise of China (1980 per capita GDP of 200 dollars → 2020 10,000 dollars).

**Financialization**: derivatives, securitization, hedge funds, LBOs, private equity. **The dot-com bubble** (1999–2001), **subprime** (2003–07) → **the 2008 financial crisis**: the Lehman bankruptcy, world GDP −2%, unemployment +5 percentage points, astronomical government bailouts.

**The lie of the Great Moderation**: 1985–2007 GDP volatility fell 50% → the confidence that "the macro is a solved problem" (Bernanke 2004) → 2008 exposed that *the model itself did not contain crisis* (the limits of RBC and DSGE).

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 3)**:
> - **Global currents**: The end of the Cold War, the rise of China, Islamism, European integration (the EU 1992), 9/11, the Iraq War, the financial crisis
> - **Great figures**: Reagan, Thatcher, Volcker, Greenspan, Deng Xiaoping, Gorbachev, Yeltsin, Putin, Clinton, Blair, Lula, Schroeder
> - **Technological revolution**: The PC, the internet, the web (1991), the mobile phone, biotechnology, financial engineering. **The internet was the *physical infrastructure* of neoliberalism**
> - **National cultures**: Anglo-American neoliberal orthodoxy, China's *socialist market economy* (Deng's 1992 southern tour), Russian oligarch capitalism, India's 1991 liberalization, the Latin American *Pink Tide* (Lula, Chavez), the rise of Islamic finance
> - **Social change**: Middle-class stagnation, the revival of inequality (Piketty's 1980 threshold), globalization's losers (the rust belt), an explosion of migration, third-wave feminism, the LGBT movement, the environmental movement in earnest

**Decisive transition**: The 2008 financial crisis was the decisive falsification of the *market-omnipotence hypothesis*. But the *political response* was paradoxical — financial bailouts + austerity + quantitative easing *preserved the existing power structure*. The entryway to the next act's *crisis of democratic legitimacy*.

### Act 4 — Quantitative easing, multipolarity, COVID, AI (2008–present)

**Quantitative easing (QE)**: Large-scale asset purchases by the Fed, the ECB, the BOJ, and the BOE (from 2008). No inflation — but *asset-price inflation* (stocks, real estate) → accelerating concentration of wealth.

**The European debt crisis** (2010–12) — IMF rescues of Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain. **Brexit** (2016), **Trump** (2016), **the Arab Spring** (2011) → the crisis of neoliberalism's political legitimacy.

**The rise of China**: GDP overtook Japan in 2010, overtook the U.S. in PPP in 2014. **BRI** (from 2013), **the digital yuan** (from 2014), **the U.S.-China trade war** (from 2018) → *G2 decoupling* in earnest.

**COVID** (from 2020): a GDP shock of −3% to −10%, a fiscal explosion (a U.S. fiscal stimulus of 25% of GDP), an MMT natural experiment, the revival of inflation (from 2021) → the Fed to 5% interest rates (from 2022).

**The AI era** (after ChatGPT in 2022): labor automation, platform consolidation, national AI competition, the alignment crisis. **The Russia-Ukraine war** (from 2022), **the revival of Middle East conflict** (from 2023), **the acceleration of the climate crisis**.

**A multipolar order in formation**: the United States, the EU, China + India, Brazil, the Islamic bloc + the AI giants (Microsoft, Google, Meta, Anthropic, OpenAI). *Nation-unit macro* competes with *corporation-unit macro*.

> **The five external-variable axes (Act 4)**:
> - **Global currents**: Multipolarity, U.S.-China decoupling, COVID, the Russia-Ukraine war, the climate crisis, the advent of AI. **All consensuses reexamined after 2008 + the end of single hegemony**
> - **Great figures**: Bernanke, Yellen, Powell, Draghi, Xi, Modi, Putin, Trump, Bezos, Musk, Altman, Hassabis, Bukele (the Bitcoin nation). **Nation + corporation + coin giants simultaneously**
> - **Technological revolution**: Mobile, blockchain, generative AI, quantum, climate technology, autonomous driving. **AI automates every industry + standardizes algorithmic governance**
> - **National cultures**: The United States (tech + inflation), the EU (regulation, climate), China (state capital, the digital yuan), India (UPI digital public infrastructure), Islam (sharia finance + digital integration), Africa (M-Pesa mobile money), Latin America (a leftward return, Bitcoin experiments)
> - **Social change**: The peak of inequality, intergenerational conflict, youth stagnation, fourth-wave feminism, climate justice, digital labor, AI jobs, basic-income experiments, Gen MZ, aging

**Decisive transition** (currently underway): the simultaneous shocks of AI + climate + the multipolar order → the *fifth variant* of the capitalist model itself is forming. *Which model will prevail* is unsolved — the domain where this note enters academic tracking mode.

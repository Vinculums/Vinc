# macroeconomics

## One-line essence
It addresses the dynamics of output, employment, prices, and interest rates of the *whole* national economy, and *effective demand, money, and expectations* are its three pillars.

## Intuitive picture
It asks not about the decision of one person but about the result of the simultaneous aggregation of the decisions of *hundreds of millions of people*. This aggregation is not simple arithmetic — a *fallacy of composition* is essentially embedded in it.

- **The fallacy of composition**: If everyone saves, *aggregate demand* falls and in the end everyone can become poorer (Keynes's paradox of thrift). Micro rationality gives rise to macro irrationality.
- **Effective demand**: A market works only *when demand supports it*. The 1929 Great Depression was a state of *supply capacity existing but no demand* — the decisive event in which classical economics's Say's law (supply creates its own demand) collapsed.
- **The two faces of money**: Money is the lubricant of transactions (*money neutrality*) and at the same time the price of capital (*money non-neutrality*). The oscillation between these two views is the axis of a 100-year macroeconomic debate.
- **Expectations**: The very *anticipation* of the future determines present behavior. The Lucas critique — since *policy change* also *changes anticipations*, one cannot predict the future from past data.
- **Business cycles**: Output *oscillates periodically* around a trend line. Whether it is a response to exogenous shocks (supply, demand, financial, policy) or intrinsic dynamics (capital accumulation, the credit cycle) is answered differently by each school.

In macroeconomics, *the models are five schools, the data are one*. The same GDP, unemployment, and inflation data are interpreted by Keynesians, monetarists, rational expectations, RBC, and New Keynesians *each with their own model*. The very multiplicity of schools is the essence of the discipline.

## Mathematical structure

**Keynes's simple macro (income-determination theory)**:
$$Y = C(Y) + I + G + (X - M)$$
$$C = c_0 + c_1 \cdot Y_d \quad (Y_d = Y - T)$$

The multiplier effect:
$$\Delta Y = \frac{1}{1 - c_1(1-t)} \cdot \Delta G$$

→ A 1-won increase in government spending increases GDP by *the multiple*. The higher $c_1$ (the marginal propensity to consume), the larger the multiplier.

**IS-LM (Hicks 1937)** — simultaneous equilibrium of the real and monetary sectors:
- IS: $Y = C(Y-T) + I(r) + G$ — goods-market equilibrium, $\partial I/\partial r < 0$
- LM: $\frac{M}{P} = L(Y, r)$ — money-market equilibrium, $\partial L/\partial r < 0$

The intersection of the two curves is the short-run equilibrium $(Y^*, r^*)$. Monetary and fiscal policy shift the curves.

**The Phillips curve** (1958, reconstructed by the Phelps-Friedman 1968 natural-rate hypothesis):
$$\pi = \pi^e - \beta(u - u^*) + \epsilon$$
- $\pi$: inflation, $\pi^e$: expected inflation, $u$: unemployment rate, $u^*$: the natural rate of unemployment
- In the short run an inflation-unemployment trade-off, in the long run vertical at the *natural rate* (the Friedman-Lucas conclusion)

**Rational expectations (Lucas 1972)**:
$$E_t[X_{t+1}] = E[X_{t+1} | \Omega_t]$$
- $\Omega_t$: *all the information* the agent has at time $t$. The average error is 0.
- → *Systematic* policy effects are powerless because agents reflect them in advance (the Policy Ineffectiveness Proposition)

**The RBC model (Kydland-Prescott 1982)** — dynamic optimization:
$$\max E_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t U(C_t, L_t)$$
$$\text{s.t.} \quad C_t + I_t = Z_t F(K_t, N_t), \quad K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)K_t + I_t$$

- $Z_t$: technology shock (TFP shock) — the *cause* of business cycles. The claim that nominal fluctuations are irrelevant and only *real shocks* are essential.
- Reduces macroeconomics to the *optimal choice of a representative agent*.

**DSGE (the modern standard)** — RBC + New Keynesian frictions (price rigidity, information frictions, financial frictions):
- New Keynesian Phillips Curve: $\pi_t = \beta E_t \pi_{t+1} + \kappa \tilde{y}_t$
- Taylor rule (1993): $i_t = r^* + \pi_t + 0.5(\pi_t - \pi^*) + 0.5 \tilde{y}_t$
- → A *quasi-automatic operating rule* for central-bank policy — the standard framework of the U.S. Fed, the ECB, and others.

## Why it matters

Macroeconomics is *the formal tool of national policy*. The money supply, interest rates, fiscal spending, exchange rates — every macro-policy decision is ultimately made on top of a single macro-model. Despite the multiplicity of schools, *which model one uses* determines the policy direction.

Deeper positioning:
- **The acceleration of micro-foundations**: Since the 1970s the macro has been micro-ized into *representative-agent optimization*. But this contradicts the origin of the macro, whose essence is the *fallacy of composition* — an internal tension of the discipline.
- **Integration with finance (after 2008)**: Bernanke's credit and financial accelerator → Reinhart-Rogoff debt dynamics → MMT (Modern Monetary Theory). Macro-finance integration is underway in earnest.
- **The limits against real markets**:
  - *The asymmetry of predictive capacity* — prediction fails *before* a crisis, and only ex-post explanation is abundant *after*
  - *Data limits* — a single country's time series = a small N, model identification is difficult
  - *Nonlinearity and systemic crisis* — a normal-times model is powerless in a crisis (the Minsky moment)
  - *The distribution problem* — the representative-agent model conceals distribution itself (Piketty's critique)
- **Policy tools**: Inflation targeting, QE, MMT, basic income, the carbon tax — the quantitative evaluation of every policy depends on a macro-model

What matters most is that new schools spring up precisely where macroeconomics collapses (2008, COVID, AI automation, climate). The same pattern as the micro (note 01) — *the model is a map of the domains where the model breaks*.

## Historical context (the R-03 economic-variant four acts + integration of the five external-variable axes)

### Act 1 — Emergence of theory (1929–1947)

**The Great Depression** (1929–1939) was the direct stimulus. Classical economics (Say's law, the self-adjusting market) could not explain *a decade of persistent unemployment and deflation*. **Keynes** (1936, *General Theory*) made explicit the concept of *effective demand* — the market may not self-adjust, and *insufficient aggregate demand* is the cause of chronic unemployment. He justified the government's active fiscal and monetary intervention.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The 1929 Great Depression + World War II (1939–45) — a *survival crisis* of capitalism itself. The Soviet planned-economy alternative rose as a *systemic rival*.
> - **Great figures**: Keynes (a Cambridge liberal, policy adviser to the British Treasury), Hayek (an Austrian liberal critic) — the earnest start of the *branching of schools*.
> - **Technological revolution**: **The emergence of macroeconometrics** (Tinbergen 1939, Frisch 1933 Nobel) — the first tool for the *formal model estimation* of macro data.
> - **National cultures**: Britain (Keynes, the LSE and Cambridge), the United States (Hansen introduced Keynes to Harvard), Germany (the parallel development of postwar *ordoliberalism*), the USSR (the Gosplan planned economy).
> - **Social change**: *Mass unemployment* spread into the labor movement, totalitarianism, and New Deal politics. Macroeconomics established itself as *a tool of political stability*.

**Decisive transition**: Keynes's *effective demand* — the paradigm was established that *aggregate variables* not reducible to micro utility maximization are essential. But the absence of micro-foundations → the next act's unsolved problem.

### Act 2 — Formalization + the golden age (1947–1970)

**The neoclassical synthesis**: **Samuelson** (*Foundations*, 1947) settled Keynes's insight in *mathematical form*. **Hicks**'s (1937) IS-LM compressed Keynes into a *graphical tool*. **Modigliani** and **Tobin** (consumption and asset theory) reinforced the micro-foundations. **Solow-Swan** (1956) growth theory — the long-run GDP determination of capital, labor, and technology.

**The postwar golden age**: 1947–1970, the United States, Western Europe, and Japan had *high growth, full employment, and low inflation*. Keynesian policy *seemed to work*. The confidence that macroeconomics was a *completed discipline*.

**The Phillips curve** (1958): The *inflation-unemployment trade-off* was found in 100 years of British data. It offered policymakers a *menu* — a tool suggesting that by tolerating a little inflation one could reduce unemployment.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The Marshall Plan, Bretton Woods, fixed exchange rates, full employment. Keynesianism was the *consensus of Western capitalism*.
> - **Great figures**: Samuelson (MIT, 1970 Nobel), Solow (1987 Nobel), Modigliani, Tobin, Hicks (1972 Nobel). The United States settled as the *world center of economics*.
> - **Technological revolution**: The advent of the computer (from the 1950s) → large-scale macroeconometric models (the Klein-Goldberger and Brookings models with 100+ equations). Macroeconomics as a *mechanized policy tool*.
> - **National cultures**: The United States (the MIT and Yale Keynesian orthodoxy), Britain (the LSE and Cambridge heirs), Germany's ordoliberalism branch, the Japanese developmental state (a micro-macro fusion).
> - **Social change**: The establishment of the welfare state (the NHS in 1948, Medicare in 1965). Keynesianism as a *tool of distributive justice*. But the Vietnam War, the civil-rights movement, and generational conflict were signs of the end.

**Decisive transition**: The stagflation of the 1970s (high inflation + high unemployment *simultaneously*) — the Phillips curve *shifted vertically* or had *entirely different dynamics*. The *predictive failure* of Keynesian policy. The paradigm shift of the next act exploded.

### Act 3 — Market verification and failure + the paradigm shift (1970–1990)

**Monetarism (Friedman, Schwartz)**: The 1963 *Monetary History of the U.S.* — the Great Depression was the *result of a contraction* of the money supply, the Fed's fault. **Friedman**'s 1968 natural-rate-of-unemployment hypothesis — the Phillips curve is *vertical in the long run*. The government's active policy *only raises expected inflation and cannot reduce unemployment*. He recommended a *fixed money-growth-rate rule*.

**The rational-expectations revolution (Lucas, Sargent, Wallace)**: **Lucas** 1972 — agents reflect *systematic policy* in advance. The government is not an *opponent* in the game but a *co-player*. The **Lucas critique** (1976) — every macroeconometric model that compares *policy alternatives* using past data is *wrong*. A *fundamental negation* of the macroeconometrics of Keynesian models.

**RBC (Kydland-Prescott 1982)**: Business cycles are not *nominal shocks* but essentially *real technology shocks*. The macro = the *dynamic optimization of a representative agent* + market clearing. Keynesian frictions and price rigidity are all *secondary*. Became the standard macroeconomic tool of the 1990s.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The 1971 collapse of Bretton Woods and the end of gold convertibility, the two oil shocks of 1973 and 1979, the 1980 Volcker disinflation, the 1989 Berlin Wall. *The dissolution of the Keynesian consensus* + *the rise of neoliberalism*.
> - **Great figures**: Friedman (1976 Nobel, Chicago), Lucas (1995), Sargent and Sims (2011), Kydland-Prescott (2004). **The Chicago school's conquest of the macro**.
> - **Technological revolution**: VAR (Sims 1980), calibration, the personal computer, dynamic-optimization algorithms. Macroeconometrics was reconstructed as a *rational-expectations-compatible* tool.
> - **National cultures**: **The U.S. Chicago became the center of the macro discipline** (Friedman, Lucas, Prescott, Sargent). Britain (Sumner, the LSE), Japan (Hayashi). Emerging economies (Korea's 1980s stabilization, China's reform and opening).
> - **Social change**: The Reagan and Thatcher governments — abandoning Keynesian policy and adopting monetarism. The weakening of labor unions and financial deregulation → the entryway to the neoliberal globalization of the 1990s.

**Decisive transition**: The Lucas critique was a *declaration of the abandonment of all macroeconometrics* — but policy authorities still needed a model. *The integration of micro-foundations + rational expectations + frictions* was the next act's point of consensus.

### Act 4 — Reinterpretation, synthesis, and the post-financial-crisis era (1990–present)

**The New Keynesian synthesis (Mankiw, Romer 1991)**: Rational expectations + price rigidity and information frictions. **Calvo** (1983) staggered pricing, the **Taylor rule** (1993). Justified Keynesian frictions with micro-foundations.

**DSGE standardization** (from the 1990s): Smets-Wouters 2003 ECB DSGE, Christiano-Eichenbaum-Evans 2005 — the standard model of every central bank. The *quasi-automatic operation* of macro policy.

**The Great Moderation** (1985–2007): The volatility of GDP and inflation halved. Bernanke 2004, "macroeconomics is a solved problem" — the peak of confidence.

**The 2008 financial crisis**: DSGE *did not have the crisis inside the model* (the representative-household and complete-financial-market assumptions → crisis is impossible). The revival of **Bernanke, Gertler, Gilchrist**'s financial accelerator, **Geanakoplos**'s leverage cycle, **Reinhart-Rogoff**'s debt-crisis dynamics, and **Minsky**'s financial-instability hypothesis.

**The latest reconstruction** (from 2008):
- **Heterogeneous agents (HANK)**: Kaplan-Moll-Violante — the distribution of wealth is essential to macro dynamics. Abandoning the *representative-agent* assumption.
- **MMT (Modern Monetary Theory)**: Wray, Kelton — a country that issues its own currency has *no fiscal constraint*. An *extreme revival* of Keynes.
- **Credit dynamics**: Schularick-Taylor 2012 — 200 years of financial-crisis data, credit explosion is a predictor of crisis.
- **Machine-learning macro forecasting**: In the ChatGPT-3 era, LLM text macro forecasting and central-bank nowcasting.
- **Climate macroeconomics**: Stern, Nordhaus, DICE — carbon and climate are essential macro-variables.

> **The five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The 2008 financial crisis, the EU debt crisis, COVID (2020) + large-scale fiscal policy, QE, the revival of inflation, Russia-Ukraine, the climate crisis, AI automation. *A reexamination of every macro consensus after 2008*.
> - **Great figures**: Bernanke (2022 Nobel, financial macro), Krugman and Stiglitz (distribution, finance), Piketty (distribution dynamics), Mian-Sufi (debt), Gabaix (behavioral macro), Mehrotra-Rey (open-economy macro). **The age of macro-finance integration**.
> - **Technological revolution**: Big data, machine-learning nowcasting, ML macro forecasting, high-frequency data, game theory + macro integration (computational economics). The limits of DSGE + new tools.
> - **National cultures**: U.S. and EU dominance + **China's alternative macro model** (the socialist market economy, state capitalism). Japan (the lost 30 years → an MMT natural experiment), emerging economies (India and Brazil macro experiments).
> - **Social change**: The revival of inequality (Piketty *Capital*, 2014), intergenerational distribution (millennials vs. baby boomers), aging, climate justice, AI jobs, basic-income experiments, digital currency (CBDC).

**Decisive transition**: After 2008, the *sole hegemony of DSGE* ended → *the revival of the multiplicity of schools*. Both the micro (note 01) and the macro are in the midst of forming a new scholarly format that accepts that *unpredictability itself is essential*.

### Core insight

The macro's four acts are a discipline of *the multiple interpretation of aggregate variables*. The same GDP, unemployment, and inflation data are all interpreted differently by Keynes (effective demand), monetarism (the money supply), rational expectations (information), RBC (technology shocks), New Keynesians (frictions), HANK (distribution), and MMT (fiscal). For 100 years, *consensus has been impossible*. In contrast to the elegance of micro (01) general equilibrium — the deepest insight is that the macro must essentially acknowledge that *the multiplicity of schools* is not a *scientific defect* but *the essence of the discipline*. Studying macroeconomics has at its core the ability to *hold five schools simultaneously* + to *evaluate the legitimacy of the choice of school in the face of the data*.

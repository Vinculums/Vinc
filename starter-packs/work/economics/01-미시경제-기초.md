# microeconomics

## One-line essence
It addresses how individual choices and market prices determine the allocation of resources, and *marginal thinking* and the *concept of equilibrium* are its two pillars.

## Intuitive picture
One consumer, one producer, and the transaction between them. From this simple picture the whole of economics springs.

- **Supply and demand**: In a market, price is a signal showing *where the winning bidders gather*. When the price rises, the number of would-be buyers falls and the number of would-be sellers grows. The point where the two curves meet is *equilibrium* — the point at which quantity traded and price are set voluntarily.
- **Marginal thinking**: "The value of one more cup vs. the cost of one more cup." Not the total but the *increment* is the unit of decision-making. This insight, discovered by Jevons, Menger, and Walras in the 1870s, turned classical economics into *modern* economics.
- **Equilibrium**: A state in which every agent has *no reason to change their current choice*. Walras's general equilibrium asks about the *whole* state in which all markets are simultaneously in equilibrium.
- **Welfare**: It asks which allocation is "better." Pareto efficiency — a state in which no change is any longer possible that would make someone happier without making anyone else less happy.
- **Game theory**: When two people's choices depend on *each other's choices*, the market model breaks down. Nash equilibrium is "the point where, when I know my opponent's strategy, I have no reason to change my own strategy."

As elegant as this model is, real markets are not elegant. *Imperfect information, externalities, public goods, market power* — the moment when everything the model assumes breaks down is in fact *most of reality*. Studying microeconomics gives equal weight to mastering the model and to seeing *where the model breaks*.

## Mathematical structure

**Supply-demand equilibrium (partial equilibrium)**:
$$Q^d(p) = Q^s(p) \implies p^* \text{ determined}$$

- $Q^d(p)$: the quantity consumers wish to buy at price $p$ — typically $\partial Q^d/\partial p < 0$
- $Q^s(p)$: the quantity producers wish to sell at price $p$ — typically $\partial Q^s/\partial p > 0$
- $p^*$: equilibrium price, $Q^*$: equilibrium quantity traded

**Utility maximization (consumer)**:
$$\max_{\{x_i\}} U(x_1, \dots, x_n) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_i p_i x_i \leq M$$

The Lagrange first-order conditions yield the *law of equal marginal utility*:
$$\frac{\partial U/\partial x_i}{p_i} = \lambda \quad \forall i \quad (\text{marginal utility per unit of money equalized})$$

→ The mathematically codified form of the marginal-utility revolution of Jevons and Menger (1870s).

**Cost minimization (producer)**:
$$\min_{\{L,K\}} wL + rK \quad \text{s.t.} \quad F(L,K) = \bar{Q}$$

→ Marginal rate of technical substitution = factor price ratio. The production function $F(L,K)$ defines the *technical combination* of capital and labor.

**General equilibrium (Walras)**: An existence theorem for a price vector $\mathbf{p}^* = (p_1^*, \dots, p_n^*)$ in which $n$ markets are *simultaneously* in equilibrium. Arrow-Debreu (1954) proved the *existence* of this vector assuming only *convexity and continuity*.

$$\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{0}$$
- $\mathbf{Z}$: excess-demand vector, Walras's law $\mathbf{p}^\top \mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{p}) = 0$ — since all value is conserved, if $n-1$ markets are in equilibrium the last market is automatically in equilibrium too.

**Welfare Theorems**:
- *First theorem*: A competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient (assuming the absence of externalities, public goods, and information asymmetry)
- *Second theorem*: Every Pareto-efficient allocation is reachable via *appropriate redistribution of initial endowments* + competitive markets

→ The basis for the legitimacy of policy. But it is nullified when many of the assumptions are violated — the entryway to the theory of market failure.

**Nash equilibrium (game theory)**:
$$\sigma_i^* \in \arg\max_{\sigma_i} u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}^*) \quad \forall i$$

- Every agent $i$ chooses the strategy that maximizes their own payoff $u_i$ when *the opponents' strategies $\sigma_{-i}^*$ are fixed*. When all are simultaneously satisfied, it is a Nash equilibrium.
- Extends to the prisoner's dilemma, coordination games, evolutionary games, and so on.

## Why it matters

Microeconomics is the micro-foundation of *the whole of economics*. Macroeconomic models too are ultimately the summation of the micro choices of a *representative agent* (DSGE, RBC). The *formal tools* of marginal thinking + the concept of equilibrium have penetrated beyond economics into political, ecological, and social models.

Deeper positioning:
- **The starting point of all subsequent theory**: Behavioral economics starts from *the limits of the utility-maximization assumption*, game theory from *the limits of perfect competition*, and information economics (Akerlof, Stiglitz) from *the limits of perfect information*
- **The bridge to macroeconomics**: Rational-expectations theory and DSGE models reconstruct the macro on top of micro-foundations
- **The limits against real markets**:
  - *Imperfect information* (lemons market, signaling games)
  - *Externalities* (Pigouvian taxes, the Coase theorem)
  - *Public goods* (the free-rider problem, Samuelson)
  - *Market power* (monopoly, oligopoly, Cournot, Bertrand)
- **Policy tools**: In the domains where the welfare theorems are violated, the *formal legitimacy* of government intervention is derived

What matters most is that subsequent schools spring up precisely where microeconomics collapses (market failure, information asymmetry, behavioral bias) — *the model is a map of the domains where the model breaks*.

## Historical context (the R-03 economic-variant four acts + integration of the five external-variable axes)

### Act 1 — Emergence of theory (the period of schools' emergence, 1776–1870)

The age of classical economics. **Adam Smith** (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776) made explicit the self-adjusting capacity of the market — the *invisible hand*. **David Ricardo** (1817) formalized comparative advantage and the labor theory of value. **John Stuart Mill** (1848) synthesized and brought classical economics to its peak. **Karl Marx** (*Das Kapital*, 1867) inverted the labor theory of value into a *critique of capitalism*.

This period was one of *absence of quantitative formalization* — value was reduced to the single measure of labor time, but the micro-mechanism of price formation was vague.

> **Note on the five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The British Industrial Revolution (from 1760) → expansion of colonial trade → the market expanded *worldwide*. For the first time economics took as its theoretical object not the *national unit* but the *world market*.
> - **Great figures**: Smith (the Scottish Enlightenment), Ricardo (from the London exchange → capacity for abstraction), Mill (a liberal statesman), Marx (Hegelian dialectics + the Rhineland social movement).
> - **Technological revolution**: The steam engine and the spinning machine → division of labor + the factory system. *Smith's pin factory* was a direct observation of the Industrial Revolution.
> - **National cultures**: Britain (free trade vs. the Corn Laws debate) → the birthplace of liberal economics. Germany offered a protectionist alternative in List's *national economics*. France had Say's law (supply creates its own demand).
> - **Social change**: The shift of power from landed aristocracy to industrial capitalists. The formation of the working class → the backdrop to Marx's emergence.

**Decisive transition**: Around 1870, the reduction of value to labor quantity was replaced by *subjective utility* and the marginal-utility revolution exploded — the *subjectivist turn* of the value-determination mechanism.

### Act 2 — Formalization (the marginal-utility revolution + the neoclassical synthesis, 1870–1930)

**The marginal-utility revolution**: **Jevons** (Britain, 1871, *Theory of Political Economy*) + **Menger** (Austria, 1871, *Grundsätze*) + **Walras** (Switzerland/France, 1874, *Éléments*) — three people *independently* discovered simultaneously the insight that value comes from marginal utility. Marginal calculus became the standard tool of economics.

**The neoclassical synthesis**: **Marshall** (*Principles of Economics*, 1890) established the supply-and-demand curves as a *visual, pedagogical* tool. *Partial equilibrium* analysis — treating a single market in isolation and discussing its equilibrium. **Walras** at the same time mathematically formalized *general equilibrium* — a price vector in which all markets are simultaneously in equilibrium (though the existence proof was incomplete).

**First-round welfare economics**: **Pigou** (1920, *Economics of Welfare*) introduced the concepts of externalities and public goods. The entryway to market-failure theory.

> **Note on the five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: The Second Industrial Revolution (electricity, chemicals, automobiles) + World War I (1914–18) → economics responded to the problem of *national resource mobilization* (the wartime economy). The shock of the 1929 Great Depression exposed the limits of *classical free-market doctrine*.
> - **Great figures**: Walras (mathematical refinement), Marshall (pedagogical synthesis), Pigou (social responsibility), Pareto (the concept of efficiency). The *subjectivist tradition* of the Austrian school (Menger → Mises → Hayek) took form.
> - **Technological revolution**: The *entry of formal tools* — calculus, set theory, general equilibrium. Economics transformed into a *mathematical science*.
> - **National cultures**: Britain (Marshall's Cambridge school), Austria (Menger and Mises's Vienna school), Switzerland/France (Walras's Lausanne school), the United States (Veblen's institutionalism) — *regional schools* were established simultaneously. The *geographic multiplicity* of the discipline began in earnest for the first time.
> - **Social change**: The spread of the labor movement + the expansion of universal suffrage → the backdrop to the emergence of welfare economics (Pigou). The *public* sphere became politically visible.

**Decisive transition**: The 1929 Great Depression → the *predictive failure* of neoclassical laissez-faire → the emergence of Keynesian macroeconomics (the entryway to the next study note, 02 macroeconomics foundations). At the same time, on the micro level, the *general-equilibrium existence theorem* remained as the next act's unsolved problem.

### Act 3 — Market verification and failure + the formal apex (1930–1970)

**Arrow-Debreu (1954)**: Completed the *existence proof* of Walrasian general equilibrium. Using topology (the Kakutani fixed-point theorem), it demonstrated that under the assumptions of convexity, continuity, and complete markets an equilibrium price vector *must exist*. The *mathematical apex* of neoclassical microeconomics.

**Formalization of the welfare theorems**: The first theorem (competitive equilibrium → Pareto efficiency), the second theorem (Pareto efficiency → reachable via appropriate redistribution + competitive markets). The *formal legitimacy* of policy was established.

**The emergence of game theory**: **von Neumann + Morgenstern** (*Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*, 1944) — cooperative games. **Nash** (1950, 1951) — the definition of the equilibrium of noncooperative games. **Schelling** (*Strategy of Conflict*, 1960) — the generalization of strategic behavior. In *the domain where perfect competition breaks down*, game theory became the standard tool.

**Market-failure theory**: **Coase** (1960, *The Problem of Social Cost*) — externalities can be resolved by *private bargaining* if transaction costs are zero. **Akerlof** (1970, *The Market for Lemons*) — information asymmetry can cause a market itself to collapse. The *systematic violation* of the welfare theorems' assumptions entered the center of the discipline in earnest.

> **Note on the five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: World War II + the Cold War + the postwar golden age (1945–70). The United States rose to be the *world center* of economics. *Military-industrial research institutes* such as the RAND Corporation and the Cowles Commission were the *financial patrons* of game theory and general equilibrium. A new dimension of the discipline's *subordination to national security*.
> - **Great figures**: Arrow and Debreu (the mathematical apex), Samuelson (textbook standardization — *Foundations*, 1947), Friedman (Chicago-school monetarism), Hayek (the revival of liberalism), Nash and Schelling (game theory).
> - **Technological revolution**: The advent of the computer (from the 1950s) → *numerical simulation* of general equilibrium became possible. Operations-research tools such as linear programming (Dantzig) and dynamic programming (Bellman) penetrated.
> - **National cultures**: The United States (Chicago vs. MIT), Britain (Hayek at the LSE vs. the Keynesian heirs at Cambridge), France (Allais), Scandinavia (Ohlin, Lindahl). The scholarly split of the *two Cold War camps* — the Eastern bloc's *planned-economy* model was on a separate track. The Japanese and East Asian *developmental-state* model (dealt with in later notes).
> - **Social change**: The expansion of the welfare state (Britain's NHS in 1948, the successors of the U.S. New Deal) → the welfare theorems were used as a *policy-justification tool*. At the same time, market-failure theory was the entryway to *criticism of the limits of the welfare state*.

**Decisive transition**: The stagflation of the 1970s (inflation + unemployment simultaneously) → the predictive failure of Keynesian macroeconomics → the *rational-expectations* revolution (Lucas) + a return to *neoclassical micro-foundations*. At the same time, on the micro level, the seeds of *behavioral economics* (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974, *Judgment under Uncertainty*).

### Act 4 — Reinterpretation and subsequent schools (1970–present)

**The behavioral-economics revolution**: **Kahneman and Tversky** (1974, 1979 *Prospect Theory*) — humans are not rational utility-maximizers. Systematic biases such as *loss aversion, anchoring, and the availability heuristic*. **Thaler** (*Nudge*, 2008) applied it to policy. An empirical exposure of the *utility-maximization assumption* of microeconomics itself.

**Mechanism design**: **Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson** — the reverse engineering of *designing markets, auctions, and voting institutions that achieve a target outcome*. An extension of general equilibrium into the *institution-design dimension*. (2007 Nobel)

**Behavioral and neuroeconomics**: **Camerer, Glimcher** — direct measurement of the neural mechanisms of decision-making. Micro-models became integrated with *psychology and neuroscience*.

**Computational and experimental economics**: **Vernon Smith** (experimental economics), **Agent-Based Modeling** (Epstein and Axtell's *Sugarscape*) — deriving macro patterns from simulation of micro-agents without general-equilibrium assumptions.

**Platform and data economics**: **Shapiro and Varian** (information economics), **Tirole** (two-sided markets, 2014 Nobel) — a new micro of market structure in the digital age. Elements absent from classical models, such as network effects and data asymmetry.

> **Note on the five external-variable axes**:
> - **Global currents**: Neoliberal globalization (from the 1980s) → the 2008 financial crisis → protectionism and platform capitalism → COVID (2020) → the AI and climate crises. Each great event revealed *violations of the assumptions* of micro-models.
> - **Great figures**: Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler (behavioral economics), Stiglitz, Akerlof (information economics), Tirole, Hart, Holmström (contracts, mechanisms), Sen, Banerjee, Duflo (development, experiments), Piketty (distribution, capital dynamics), Acemoglu (institutions, political economy).
> - **Technological revolution**: Computing + big data + machine learning → the *revolution in experimental economics, natural experiments, and causal inference* (Angrist, Imbens, Card, 2021 Nobel). The internet, platforms, blockchain, and generative AI transform *market structure itself*.
> - **National cultures**: The United States (Chicago and MIT dominance), Europe (TSE, LSE), China (the *socialist market economy* variant after reform and opening), India (development economics in earnest), Korea and Japan (the developmental-state model). The *non-Western multiplication* of the discipline began in earnest.
> - **Social change**: Widening inequality (Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, 2014) → the revival of distribution and political economy. #MeToo, BLM, and other *identity politics* → models of discrimination and fairness (Bohnet, Bertrand). Population aging, the climate crisis, and AI transform the very definition of the micro-agent.

**Decisive transition**: The integration of behavioral, experimental, and computational economics → the *turn of economics into an empirical science*. But its *predictive capacity* is still limited — the ex-ante prediction of great events such as the 2008 financial crisis, COVID, and the AI shock failed. Microeconomics is undergoing the *refinement of the model* and the *recognition of the model's limits* at the same time.

### Core insight

The essential current of microeconomics's four acts is the simultaneous progression of two axes: *the elegance of the model and the acceleration of the model's limits*. Smith's invisible hand → the general-equilibrium apex of Walras and Arrow-Debreu → the *exposure of assumption violations* by behavioral, information, and game theory → the *empirical reconstruction* by experimental, computational, and neuroeconomics. The model becomes more refined in each act, and at the same time in each act reveals *the model's inadequacy over more domains*. This is the essence of a complex-systems discipline — *unpredictability* is not a defect of the model but an *essential property of the object*.

## Self-identification of new unsolved problems (U-14 format)

Unsolved problems and limitations identified while writing this note (economic application of a format learned in the quantum-gravity domain proper):

1. **The unrealism of general equilibrium**: Arrow-Debreu's assumptions of *complete markets, complete information, and convexity* are violated in almost all real markets. *Why do we teach a model that is violated* is unsolved — the balance between value as a formal tool vs. empirical inadequacy
2. **The status of the utility-maximization assumption**: Even though the biases exposed by behavioral economics are pervasive, the model's starting point is still rational utility maximization. There is no scholarly consensus between *discarding the rationality assumption vs. refining it*
3. **The quantification of externalities and public goods**: Even 60 years after Pigou and Coase, the *accurate measurement of the social cost* of an externality is still difficult (the social cost of carbon emissions, a 10-fold difference between MIT's and the UK government's estimates)
4. **The boundary between market and politics**: The welfare theorems only say that a *competitive market* is efficient; they are silent on *which domains should be left to the market*. There is no scholarly consensus in *boundary domains* such as healthcare, education, and housing
5. **Modeling unpredictability itself**: The ex-ante prediction of the 2008, COVID, and AI shocks failed. Whether *unpredictability* is a limit of the discipline or the essence of the object is unsolved — this note adopts the *latter* hypothesis, but the verification is incomplete

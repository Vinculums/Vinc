## ② Literary Theory Foundations

### Rhetoric
- **Aristotle's 3 appeals**: ethos (character), pathos (emotion), logos (logic)
- **5 parts**: inventio (invention), dispositio (arrangement), elocutio (expression), memoria, pronuntiatio
- **Rhetorical figures**: metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, hyperbole, paradox, repetition, antithesis, irony
- **Prosody**: poetic meter (stress, quantity, syllable count), rhyme, end rhyme, alliteration

### Style
- **Noun–verb ratio**, sentence length, lexical diversity, syntactic complexity — Halliday, stylistics
- **Point of view (POV)**: first, second, third person; omniscient, limited, embedded
- **Free indirect discourse**: Flaubert, Joyce — the core of modernism

### Narrative
- **Genette's *Narrative Discourse* (1972)**: 5 categories — order, duration, frequency, mood, voice
- **Greimas's actants**: subject, object, sender, receiver, helper, opponent — 6 actants
- **Propp's *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928)**: 31 functions (an analysis of Russian folktales)
- **Campbell's *Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949)**: the hero's journey (a direct influence on Star Wars)
- **Bakhtin's chronotope** (the time-space unity): time-space modes by genre

### Genre
- **Poetry**: lyric, epic, dramatic — Aristotle
- **The novel**: romance, novel (Cervantes, 1605), narrative, confession, epistolary, picaresque, Bildungsroman
- **Drama**: tragedy, comedy, tragicomedy
- **Essay and criticism**: Montaigne (1580), Bacon
- **Modernist genre fusion**: poetry-novel (Joyce), novel-essay (Borges, Sebald), poetry-drama (Eliot's *Waste Land*)

## ④ Literary Theory in Full Scale

### Russian Formalism (1915–1930)
- **Shklovsky**, *Art as Technique* (1917): **defamiliarization (ostranenie)** — the essence of art is *making strange*. Formal devices that break automated perception
- Jakobson — the poetic function: the message focused on itself
- Tynjanov — literary evolution and system
- Eichenbaum — the defense of formalism

### New Criticism (1930–1960, Anglo-American)
- Ransom, Brooks, Wimsatt, Beardsley
- **Close reading** — focusing on the text itself, excluding author, history, and reader
- The *intentional fallacy* + the *affective fallacy*

### Bakhtin (Soviet, 1920s–30s)
- **dialogism**: every utterance is a dialogue with other utterances
- **heteroglossia**: multiple languages (the essence of the novel)
- **chronotope**: the time-space unity
- **carnival**: the overturning of hierarchy — the Rabelais analysis

### Structuralism (1960s, France)
- **Barthes**, *S/Z* (1970), *Mythologies* (1957), *The Death of the Author* (1967)
- **Lévi-Strauss**, the structure of myth (binary oppositions)
- **Genette**, *Narrative Discourse* (1972)
- **Greimas**, actants
- **Todorov**, fantastic literature

### Post-structuralism and Deconstruction (1970s–1990s)
- **Derrida**, *Of Grammatology* (1967), *Writing and Difference* — *différance*, logocentrism, deconstruction
- **de Man**, *Allegories of Reading* (1979)
- **Foucault**, *The Order of Things*, *What Is an Author?* (1969) — discourse, power, the author-function
- **Kristeva**, intertextuality

### The Frankfurt School + Marxism
- Adorno; Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1936) — aura, reproduction
- Lukács, Goldmann
- Williams, Eagleton — British cultural materialism

### Feminism, Postcolonialism, Identity
- Showalter, Gilbert, Gubar — feminist criticism
- Said's *Orientalism* (1978), Spivak's *Can the Subaltern Speak?* (1988), Bhabha — postcolonialism
- Hall, Gilroy — cultural studies
- Queer theory: Sedgwick, Butler

### Comparative Literature and Translation Studies
- **Auerbach**, *Mimesis* (1946) — a canonical analysis of Western literary history
- **Damrosch**, *What Is World Literature?* (2003)
- **Spivak**; **Apter**, *Against World Literature* (2013) — untranslatability
- **Venuti**, *The Translator's Invisibility* (1995) — making the translator visible
- **Berman**, *L'épreuve de l'étranger* — preserving foreignness

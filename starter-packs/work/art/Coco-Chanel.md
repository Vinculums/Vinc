# Coco Chanel

## ① One-line essence

**"Luxury must be comfortable, otherwise it is not luxury"** (Chanel) — replacing the corset- and ornament-excess women's dress of the Belle Époque with *functionalist simplicity* (jersey material · the little black dress · the suit) → **liberating 20th-century women's dress + founding the lineage of minimalist fashion**. Yet a two-sided figure whose WWII Nazi-collaboration controversy (see ②) must be stated alongside.

## ② Life and historical context (4 axes)

- **Life**: 1883 Saumur (Loire) — 1971 Paris, Hôtel Ritz. Peddler father · laundress mother — after her mother's death in 1895, the Aubazine convent orphanage (age 12) — *learning to sew + the convent's black-and-white, austere aesthetic* (read by some as the prototype of her later design). Nicknamed "Coco" during her cabaret-singer years. Patronage of Étienne Balsan · Arthur "Boy" Capel (financial backing · his 1919 accidental death — a lifelong loss) → 1910 the millinery shop *Chanel Modes* at 21 rue Cambon, Paris → 1913 Deauville · 1915 Biarritz boutiques → rapid growth with jersey garments during WWI → 1921 *No.5* → 1926 the little black dress → the peak of Paris couture in the 1920s–30s (exchange with artists — stage-costume collaborations with Diaghilev · Stravinsky · Cocteau · Picasso). **1939 closure of the couture house → residence at the Ritz during WWII, a relationship with the German counterintelligence (Abwehr) officer Hans Günther von Dincklage · registration as an Abwehr agent (F-7124, code name "Westminster") · records of involvement in the 1943 Operation Modellhut — also an attempt to seize the Jewish Wertheimer family's stake in Parfums Chanel by exploiting the Nazi Aryanization laws. Postwar arrest · after release, effectively about eight years of exile in Switzerland** (documented in works such as Hal Vaughan's *Sleeping with the Enemy*, 2011 — not to be whitewashed). 1954 (age 71) comeback collection — panned in Paris but revived by the support of American Vogue → global establishment of the Chanel suit → death in 1971. In 1983 Karl Lagerfeld revived the house.
- **Cognition**: *body-movement-centered cognition* — grasping clothing not as ornament but as a *tool of movement* ("clothing has meaning only when the body moves"). A pivoting cognition that extracts functional codes from menswear · workwear · sportswear
- **Evolution**: Belle Époque corsets · the S-curve (Worth · Doucet) → Poiret's removal of the corset (anticipated in the 1900s) → **Chanel completes that removal as an *everyday-dress system*** → the 1920s garçonne (boyish) era → 1947 Dior's New Look (return of the waist — explicitly opposed by Chanel: "clothing that imprisons women again") → her 1954 comeback counterattacks — an axis of the 20th-century struggle over the women's silhouette
- **Culture**: WWI (women's entry into labor = demand for functional dress) · the 1920s new woman (flapper · suffrage) · modernism (removal of ornament — contemporaneously coherent with Loos's *Ornament and Crime*, 1908) — fashion as the material medium of women's social change
- **Society**: an orphanage-born woman, through patronage (male capital) → building her own empire — an exceptional case of dual ascent in class and gender. Yet that ascent narrative coexists with records of wartime collaboration and antisemitic remarks — the two sides of *the designer of liberation + the collaborator* in a single figure

## ③ Major works and methodology

**Representative works (designs · products)**:
- **Jersey garments** (1916~) — repurposing a knit fabric used for men's underwear into women's outerwear — no corset needed · following the body · elevating a cheap material (an inventive turn from wartime material shortage)
- ***Chanel No.5*** (1921, composed by Ernest Beaux) — the first mainstream perfume to fully use aldehydes · an *abstract scent* rather than a reproduction of flowers — the first perfume bearing a couturier's name = founding the fusion of the fashion and perfume industries
- **The little black dress (LBD)** (1926) — named "Chanel's Ford" by American Vogue — turning the *black of mourning and maid's dress* into a universal uniform — simple · reproducible · stripped of class marking
- **The Chanel suit** (1920s prototype → completed from 1954) — tweed · a collarless jacket · a chain hem · practical pockets — the uniform of women's labor and mobility
- **Costume jewelry** (1920s~) — overturning the real/fake hierarchy — "jewelry is not proof of wealth but style"
- **The *2.55* quilted chain bag** (Feb 1955) — the shoulder strap = the freeing of both hands (applying the functionalist principle to the bag)
- Other: the Breton stripe · two-tone shoes · popularizing the suntan — intervening across *everyday body culture*
- Stage: costumes for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes *Le Train Bleu* (1924) — collaboration with Picasso (stage curtain) · Cocteau (libretto) (a Diaghilev-mediated return in influence graph v1 §2-2)

**Distinctive methodology**:
- **Functionalist simplicity**: removal of ornament = design — "Elegance is refusal (L'élégance, c'est le refus)". The fashion version of a contemporaneous parallel with architectural modernism (Loos · Bauhaus)
- **Appropriation of material**: jersey (underwear) · tweed (men's hunting dress) · black (mourning) — transposing *cheap · male · labor codes* into high-end women's dress
- **Priority of bodily liberation**: abolishing the corset · pockets · shoulder straps — the design criterion is *possibility of movement* rather than silhouette
- **Affirmation of reproducibility**: accepting the "Ford" nickname for the LBD — aiming at a *universal uniform* instead of the uniqueness of haute couture (coherent with modernist mass-production aesthetics)
- **Total brand**: clothing + perfume + jewelry + bag = a single sign system (the double-C logo) — founding the model of the modern luxury brand

## ④ 🔴 Cross-field influence and creation (MM-13)

#### Menswear and workwear codes → Chanel (T9 borrowing)
- **What**: jersey (men's underwear) · tweed (the Duke of Westminster's hunting dress) · sailor dress · riding dress → women's everyday wear
- **Path**: 1910s Deauville resort (observing men's sportswear) → 1916 jersey collection → 1920s tweed (direct import of Scottish material during her relationship with the Duke of Westminster)
- **Transformation**: **T9 borrowing** — but not cross-cultural, rather cross-*gender and cross-class* borrowing — a case where transferring male-appropriated functional codes to women became itself a device of liberation (a structure different from the ethical problem of Picasso's 1907 T9)
- **Basis**: 1916 *Harper's Bazaar* publication of a jersey dress / historical material on tweed from the Chanel–Westminster period

#### Poiret → Chanel (T7 + T4)
- **What**: removal of the corset — anticipated by Poiret (1900s) → completed by Chanel
- **Path**: Poiret's high waist from 1906 (a corset-free silhouette — but with excess ornament · Orientalism) → Chanel from the 1910s redefining it through *simplicity* → a public war of words between the two (Poiret: "What are you in mourning for?" — Chanel: "For you, monsieur")
- **Transformation**: **T7 convergence + T4 critical rejection** — sharing the goal of liberation · the aesthetics in head-on opposition (ornament ↔ removal). Poiret's 1929 bankruptcy ↔ Chanel's empire — recorded as a victory narrative of modernism
- **Basis**: Poiret's *En habillant l'époque* (1930) / historical anecdote of the war of words

#### Chanel → 20th-century liberation of women's dress (T2 + social change)
- **What**: abolishing the corset · active dress · pockets · uniformization — transforming the norms of the women's body through fashion
- **Path**: 1916 jersey → 1926 LBD → 1954 suit → the standard dress of the career woman (combined with the entry into office labor from the 1960s) → Yves Saint Laurent's *Le Smoking* (1966 — the women's tuxedo, a direct descendant of Chanel's menswear-borrowing line)
- **Timing**: 1916–1971 (a half-century of sustained intervention)
- **Transformation**: **T2 stylistic inheritance (the entire succession)** — yet Chanel herself was unconnected to the feminist movement · panned the 1960s miniskirt (a conservative turn) — a distinction is needed between *resulting liberation ≠ intentional feminism*
- **Basis**: the 1926 Vogue "Ford" article / Valerie Steele's *Paris Fashion* (1988) / YSL 1966

#### Chanel → the minimalist-fashion lineage (T2)
- **What**: "elegance = refusal" — the aesthetic of removal → late-20th-century to contemporary minimalism
- **Path**: Chanel's simplicity → 1970s Halston (American minimalism) → the uniform aesthetic of Jil Sander · Calvin Klein · Prada in the 1980s–90s → Phoebe Philo (Céline) in the 2010s "clothes women actually wear" — a direct restatement of Chanel's functionalist principle → the discourse of quiet luxury
- **Transformation**: **T2 stylistic inheritance** — the Japanese lineage (Rei Kawakubo · Yamamoto's black · deconstruction) converges by a separate path (T7 — a follow-up connection for the v12 fashion placement)
- **Basis**: the Chanel references in Sander and Philo interviews / Steele's *Minimalism* discourse

#### Chanel No.5 → crossing perfume · industry · art (T6)
- **What**: abstract scent + couturier branding → the 20th-century industrial and cultural model of perfume
- **Path**: 1921 No.5 → Parfums Chanel (1924, Wertheimer capital — later the object of the attempted Aryanization seizure) → Marilyn Monroe's 1952 interview ("a few drops of No.5" as her nightwear) → **Andy Warhol's *Ads: Chanel* (1985) silkscreen — turning the perfume bottle into a pop-art icon**
- **Transformation**: **T6 medium translation** (fashion → scent → advertising image → pop art) — the cross-field iconization of a single product
- **Basis**: Warhol's *Ads* series (1985) / Monroe's 1952 *Life* interview

#### Lagerfeld → reinterpretation of the Chanel house (T8)
- **What**: 1971 death · stagnation of the house → 1983 Karl Lagerfeld as creative director — a postmodern recombination of the Chanel codes (tweed · chain · camellia · double-C)
- **Transformation**: **T8 reinterpretation · rediscovery** — founding the model of *archiving + reinterpreting a brand's codes* after the founder's death — thereafter the standard strategy of the entire luxury industry (the successor-director regime of Dior · Gucci, etc.). A *commercial version*, paralleling the 1829 revival of Bach and the 1970s rediscovery of Hildegard
- **Basis**: Lagerfeld's 1983–2019 collections / studies of house-archive strategy

**Fashion → other fields**:
- **Design · architecture**: the principle of removing ornament converges contemporaneously with the functionalism of Loos · Bauhaus (T7) — a case proving fashion an equal field of the modernist movement
- **Branding · management**: the total brand (clothing + perfume + logo) — the prototype of the very structure of the modern luxury industry
- **Film**: Audrey Hepburn's LBD (*Breakfast at Tiffany's*, 1961 — produced by Givenchy, but the LBD norm is Chanel's) — the film iconization of a costume code

## ⑤ Answers to the meta-questions

- **Q1 (why define beauty?)**: beauty = *freedom of the body* — "fashion fades, but style remains". The answer that beauty is a tool of life rather than an accumulation of ornament — a fashion formalization of functionalist aesthetics
- **Q2 (by what criterion?)**: *removal (refus) + function + repeatability*. Replacing the unique haute-couture criterion with a *universal-uniform* criterion — the exact opposite of Bach's formal elaboration (the peak of addition): a *peak of subtraction*
- **Q3 (cross-field influence and creation)**: within fashion — a triple founding of women's-dress liberation + the minimalist lineage + the luxury-brand model. Across fields — perfume · pop art · branding · film. Yet she raises for art-historical methodology the problem of separating a figure's ethics (wartime collaboration) from her field contribution (isomorphic with the problem of evaluating Wagner's antisemitism)

## ⑥ Connections and successors

- Forerunners: Charles Frederick Worth (the institution of haute couture) · Paul Poiret (the earlier removal of the corset) · the Aubazine convent aesthetic
- Contemporaries: Elsa Schiaparelli (a lifelong rival — surrealist ornament ↔ Chanel's removal) · Madeleine Vionnet (bias cut) · Diaghilev · Picasso · Stravinsky · Cocteau (the 1920s Paris collaboration network)
- Direct successors: Yves Saint Laurent (*Le Smoking* 1966) · Halston · Jil Sander · Calvin Klein · Phoebe Philo
- House succession: Karl Lagerfeld (1983–2019) → Virginie Viard → present
- Opposition · convergence: Dior's New Look (the 1947 antipode) · Rei Kawakubo · Yohji Yamamoto (a separate path of black · deconstruction — a connection target for the v12 fashion placement)
- Critical literature: Hal Vaughan's *Sleeping with the Enemy* (2011) · Rhonda Garelick's *Mademoiselle* (2014) — documenting the collaboration record

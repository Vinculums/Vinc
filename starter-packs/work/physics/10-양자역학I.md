# Quantum Mechanics

## One-line essence
Quantum mechanics is the theory that treats physical quantities as operators and states as Hilbert-space vectors, and in which measurement creates essential randomness — it follows automatically once you replace the Poisson bracket of the Hamiltonian formalism with $\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]$.

## Physical picture
Getting the intuition through **four decisive experiments**:

1. **Blackbody radiation (1900)**: classical theory predicts an ultraviolet divergence (the Rayleigh-Jeans catastrophe). When Planck quantizes energy in units of $E = h\nu$, it fits exactly. The beginning of the quantum.
2. **Photoelectric effect (1905)**: even weak light emits electrons if the frequency is high enough. Light is a bundle of particles called photons.
3. **Double slit (single-particle version)**: even firing photons or electrons one at a time, an interference pattern builds up cumulatively. A particle interferes with itself. **Put a detector at the slit and the pattern disappears** — measurement changes the state.
4. **Stern-Gerlach (1922)**: a beam of silver atoms in an inhomogeneous magnetic field. The classical prediction is a continuous distribution, the reality is exactly two points. Quantization of angular momentum. The discovery of spin.

**The key intuition**:
- Classical: a particle is at one position, and measurement finds out that position.
- Quantum: a particle is in a coherent superposition of all possible states, and measurement collapses it to one possibility — that probability is the squared modulus of the amplitude.

**Analogy**: a spinning coin is neither heads nor tails but both. The moment you cover it with your hand and look, it is decided to one side. In the quantum realm this is not an analogy but reality. The decisive difference: for the coin, the outcome is "already" fixed even while spinning (hidden variables). The quantum is not so — the violation of Bell's inequality proves it.

**Entanglement**: couple two particles quantum-mechanically and a measurement on one immediately determines the outcome of the other — regardless of distance. It is not information transmission, but the correlation exceeds the speed of light (defying classical intuition). The EPR paradox → Bell's inequality → the Aspect experiment → the age of quantum information.

**Uncertainty**: $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$. Not a measurement limit but the essence of the state — a state with exact position and exact momentum does not exist.

## Mathematical structure
**Four postulates** (von Neumann 1932):

1. **State**: a unit vector $|\psi\rangle$ in the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ (phase-independent)
2. **Observable**: a self-adjoint operator $\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$. Its eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes
3. **Born rule**: the probability of measuring the eigenstate $|a_n\rangle$ is $P(a_n) = |\langle a_n|\psi\rangle|^2$
4. **Time evolution**: the Schrödinger equation
$$i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle$$

**Position-representation Schrödinger equation**:
$$i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t} = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\vec{r})\right]\psi$$

**Canonical quantization** (the full-fledged use of the connection recognized in Hamiltonian Mechanics):
$$\{f, g\}_{\text{PB}} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{f}, \hat{g}]$$

**Canonical commutation relation** — from this single line all of quantum mechanics follows:
$$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$$

**Uncertainty principle** (Robertson-Schrödinger):
$$\Delta A \, \Delta B \geq \frac{1}{2}|\langle [\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle|$$
Position-momentum: $\Delta x \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$

**Heisenberg picture** (the operators carry the time evolution) — directly parallel to Hamiltonian Mechanics:
$$i\hbar \frac{d\hat{A}}{dt} = [\hat{A}, \hat{H}]$$
Compare: classical $\frac{df}{dt} = \{f, H\}$. Visible evidence of canonical quantization.

**Measurement projection**:
$$|\psi\rangle \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\hat{P}_a |\psi\rangle}{\|\hat{P}_a |\psi\rangle\|}$$
This projection (collapse) is the epicenter of the measurement problem.

**Composite systems — the tensor product**:
$$\mathcal{H}_{AB} = \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$$
**Entanglement**: $|\Psi\rangle \neq |\phi_A\rangle \otimes |\phi_B\rangle$ (not separable into a tensor product)

**Bell state** (maximal entanglement):
$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$$

**Feynman path integral** (1948, directly connected to Lagrangian Mechanics):
$$\langle x_f, t_f | x_i, t_i\rangle = \int \mathcal{D}x\, e^{iS[x]/\hbar}$$
The action $S$ enters as a phase in units of $\hbar$. In the classical limit $\hbar \to 0$, the variational principle (the least-action path) prevails.

**Three key solvable models** (the building blocks of all quantum applications):
- 1D infinite well: $E_n = n^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2/(2mL^2)$
- Harmonic oscillator: $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + \tfrac{1}{2})$, zero-point energy $\hbar\omega/2$
- Hydrogen atom: $E_n = -13.6\,\text{eV}/n^2$ (3D Coulomb + angular momentum)

## Why it matters
**Solving every microscopic problem that classical physics could not**:
- Blackbody, photoelectric, Compton (the photon) / atomic stability / discrete spectra / chemical bonding and the periodic table / specific heat and magnetism of solids / superconductivity and superfluidity

**The foundation of all theories of matter**: atoms, molecules, solids, semiconductors. The single theory dealing with the microscopic structure of our universe.

**Technological applications — the foundation of the late-20th-century industrial revolution**:
- Semiconductors (transistors, CPUs, memory)
- Lasers (optical-fiber communication, medicine, precision measurement)
- MRI, PET (quantum nuclear spin, particle detection)
- The optical atomic clock of GPS (the quantum-transition standard)
- Quantum information, computing, and cryptography (BB84, Shor, Grover)

**Bridges to higher theories**:
- **QM + SR = quantum field theory**: Dirac 1928 → QED → the Standard Model (all particle theories)
- **QM + statistical mechanics = quantum statistics**: Bose-Einstein, Fermi-Dirac, $Z = \text{Tr}\, e^{-\beta\hat{H}}$
- **QM + GR = quantum gravity (unsolved)**: string theory, loop quantum gravity, holography

**The measurement problem — unsolved for 100 years**:
- Copenhagen (Bohr): collapse upon measurement is a basic property of the universe
- Many-worlds (Everett 1957): no collapse, every possibility is a parallel universe
- Decoherence (Zeh, Zurek): entanglement with the environment explains the emergence of classicality
- Bohm: nonlocal hidden variables, restoring determinism

The interpretation is undetermined, but the mathematical formalism and predictions agree 100%. The 12-digit agreement of $g-2$ makes it the most accurate theory humankind has made.

## Historical context
**Act I — the old quantum theory (1900–1924)**:
- 1900 Planck — the blackbody-radiation quantum hypothesis $E = h\nu$. He himself regarded it as a "mathematical trick"
- 1905 Einstein — the photon via the photoelectric effect (1921 Nobel)
- 1913 Bohr — the quantized model of the hydrogen atom ($L = n\hbar$)
- 1923 Compton — the photon is a particle carrying momentum
- 1924 de Broglie — wave nature $\lambda = h/p$ for every particle. It was a doctoral thesis, but Einstein recommended it

**Act II — the burst of real quantum mechanics (1925–1928)**:
- June 1925 Heisenberg — matrix mechanics on the island of Helgoland. "Let us deal only with observable quantities"
- September 1925 Born-Jordan — formalization of matrix mechanics, the first derivation of $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$
- 1925 Pauli — the exclusion principle. A year later extended to spin
- **January 1926 Schrödinger** — wave mechanics. Starting from the de Broglie wave, he derived the Schrödinger equation. **A different formalism appeared almost simultaneously with Heisenberg's.** Schrödinger himself proved the equivalence of the two formalisms
- 1926 Born — the probabilistic interpretation of $|\psi|^2$ (first appearing in a footnote, 1954 Nobel)
- 1927 Heisenberg — the uncertainty principle
- 1927 Solvay Conference — the Einstein-Bohr debate. "God does not play dice"
- 1928 Dirac — the Dirac equation (unifying SR+QM). The prediction of the positron → Anderson's 1932 discovery

**Act III — the formalization and interpretation debates (1932–)**:
- 1932 von Neumann — publication of *Mathematical Foundations*. An axiom system on Hilbert space
- 1935 EPR — "Quantum mechanics is incomplete." Questioning nonlocal correlations
- 1935 Schrödinger — the cat thought experiment. Introduced the term "entanglement" (Verschränkung)
- 1948 Feynman — the path integral. A direct connection between the Lagrangian and quantum mechanics
- 1957 Everett — the many-worlds interpretation
- 1964 Bell — Bell's inequality. Turning EPR into an experimentally testable proposition
- 1982 Aspect — the first precision verification of Bell violation → **the death of local hidden variables**
- 1980– Zurek, Joos — decoherence theory, the mechanism of the emergence of classicality
- **2022 Nobel** — Aspect, Clauser, Zeilinger (quantum entanglement experiments)

**The key insight**: quantum mechanics is not one person's invention but **a 30-year collective work**. Heisenberg's abstract matrices and Schrödinger's waves appeared almost simultaneously from different starting points and were then unified. A rare period in which experiment forced theory, rather than theory chasing experiment.

# Lagrangian Mechanics

## One-line essence
Nature chooses the single path, among all possible paths, for which the action is minimized.

## Physical picture
When you throw a ball from A to B, what curve is traced? In the Newtonian way, the answer is "since gravity acts at every instant, integrating the acceleration gives a parabola." In the Lagrangian way it is different.

Imagine **every possible path** going from A to B — a straight line, a zigzag, even a path that loops all the way around. For each path a certain number (the action $S$) is determined. Nature picks out precisely the path for which this number is smallest. It is the generalization of the fact that light, when traveling between two points, always takes the fastest route (Fermat's principle).

Analogy: lazy nature. It does not needlessly waste energy. It takes the route where the accumulation of "the difference between kinetic and potential energy at each instant" is minimal.

The key shift:
- Newton: the **local, causal** view of "force at each instant → change in motion"
- Lagrange: the **global, teleological** view of "optimization of the whole path"
They give the same answer, but the viewpoints are completely different.

## Mathematical structure
**The Lagrangian**:
$$L(q, \dot{q}, t) = T - V$$
- $T$: kinetic energy, $V$: potential energy, $q$: generalized coordinate (angle, length, any degree of freedom)

**The action**:
$$S[q(t)] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q, \dot{q}, t)\, dt$$

**Principle of least action**: the actual path is the one for which $\delta S = 0$.

From this variational condition, the **Euler-Lagrange equation** emerges:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0$$

The meaning of each term:
- $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}$ : the generalized momentum $p_i$
- $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}$ : the generalized force
- the difference of the two terms = 0 → the rate of change of momentum equals the force (the generalization of Newton's F=dp/dt)

**Noether's theorem**:
$$L \text{ is symmetric with respect to } q_i \implies p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \text{ is conserved}$$

The one-to-one correspondence between symmetry and conserved quantity becomes clear here.

## Why it matters
Lagrangian mechanics is **the common language of physics**. Newtonian mechanics is tied to Cartesian coordinates, but the Lagrangian is used as-is in **any coordinate system**. The angle of a pendulum, the Euler angles of a rotating body, the spacetime coordinates of general relativity — all take the identical form.

More decisive reasons:
- **A bridge to quantum mechanics**: Feynman's path integral $\langle f|i\rangle = \int \mathcal{D}q\, e^{iS/\hbar}$ — the action is the core of the quantum world
- **The foundation of field theory**: generalizing the particle coordinate $q(t)$ → the field $\phi(x,t)$ brings electromagnetism, quantum field theory, and general relativity all within one formalism
- **The language of the Standard Model**: every interaction in particle physics is written in a single line of Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L}$
- **Clarification of symmetry**: gauge symmetry and Lorentz symmetry are all naturally expressed on top of the Lagrangian

If Newton saw "force" as the starting point, Lagrange saw "energy and symmetry" as the starting point. The latter is deeper.

## Historical context
In the late 18th century, Euler and Lagrange were developing the calculus of variations — the mathematics that treats a function itself as the variable and deals with the optimization of that function.

Lagrange (1788, *Mécanique Analytique*) completely rewrote Newtonian mechanics. His boast was "this book contains not a single figure" — he showed that everything could be handled analytically.

Background puzzles:
- In systems with complicated constraints (e.g., the string of a pendulum, a sliding slope), Newton's equations are messy because the constraint forces must be handled one by one
- Problems like rotation, oscillation, and coupled pendulums, for which Cartesian coordinates are unsuitable
- The Lagrangian method **automatically eliminates the constraint forces** and **leaves only the degrees of freedom**

Hamilton (1834) reorganized this as a variational principle, completing the formalism "action = time-integrated Lagrangian." A century after this, Feynman took it over directly into quantum mechanics.

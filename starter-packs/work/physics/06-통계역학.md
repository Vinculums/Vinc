# Statistical Mechanics

## One-line essence
The bridge that explains how macroscopic properties such as temperature, pressure, and magnetism emerge from the microscopic laws of $10^{23}$ particles.

## Physical picture
A single glass of water in a cup contains about $10^{25}$ molecules. Tracking the position and velocity of every molecule is impossible — yet we describe that water's temperature, pressure, and volume in a single line. How is this possible?

The answer: **give up the individual information and view it statistically**. We do not know how each molecule moves. But we do know "what the distribution of the total energy looks like." That is the starting point of statistical mechanics.

The key intuitions:
- **Ergodic hypothesis**: time average = ensemble average. The average from tracking one particle for a long time equals the average over many systems under the same conditions
- **Boltzmann distribution**: at equilibrium at temperature $T$, the probability of a state with energy $E$ appearing $\propto e^{-E/k_BT}$. The colder it is, the more they crowd into low energy; the hotter it is, the more evenly they spread
- **Phase transition**: as you slowly change a parameter (temperature, etc.), at some moment a macroscopic property jumps **discontinuously**. Like water suddenly becoming ice at 0°C. This is rigorously defined only in the limit of an infinite number of particles

Analogy: throw a million coins and the fraction of heads comes out almost exactly 50%. The law of large numbers. Statistical mechanics is this law applied to physics.

## Mathematical structure
**Boltzmann distribution (canonical ensemble)**:
$$P_i = \frac{e^{-E_i/k_BT}}{Z}$$
- $P_i$: the probability of being in microstate $i$
- writing $\beta = 1/(k_BT)$, $P_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$

**Partition function**:
$$Z = \sum_i e^{-E_i/k_BT}$$
- the weighted sum over all microstates — the **most central object** of statistical mechanics
- every macroscopic physical quantity is derived from $Z$

**Deriving macroscopic quantities (the magic dictionary)**:
$$F = -k_B T \ln Z \quad \text{(Helmholtz free energy)}$$
$$\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}$$
$$S = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} = k_B(\ln Z + \beta \langle E \rangle)$$
$$\langle (\Delta E)^2 \rangle = \frac{\partial^2 \ln Z}{\partial \beta^2} \quad \text{(fluctuation-response theorem)}$$

**Kinds of ensemble**:
- **Microcanonical**: energy fixed ($N, V, E$ fixed) → $S = k_B \ln \Omega$
- **Canonical**: temperature fixed ($N, V, T$ fixed) → Boltzmann distribution
- **Grand canonical**: particle number also varies ($\mu, V, T$) → essential for quantum statistics (Fermi, Bose)

**Quantum statistics** (indistinguishable particles):
$$\bar{n}_i = \frac{1}{e^{(E_i - \mu)/k_BT} \pm 1}$$
- $+$ Fermi-Dirac (electrons, protons) — the Pauli exclusion principle
- $-$ Bose-Einstein (photons, helium-4) — Bose-Einstein condensation

**Phase transitions and critical phenomena**:
$$\xi \sim |T - T_c|^{-\nu}, \quad C \sim |T - T_c|^{-\alpha}$$
- $\xi$: correlation length (diverges at the critical point)
- $T_c$: critical temperature
- $\alpha, \nu$: critical exponents — classified into universality classes independently of microscopic detail

**Ising model (the simplest phase-transition model)**:
$$H = -J \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} s_i s_j - h\sum_i s_i, \quad s_i \in \{-1, +1\}$$
- the standard model of the ferromagnetic phase transition
- the 2D solution was solved by Onsager (1944), and it was revealed that the critical exponents differ from mean-field theory

## Why it matters
Statistical mechanics is **a theory that explains the very existence of matter**. Why water freezes at 0°C and boils at 100°C, why some substances become magnets and others do not, why nuclear fusion occurs inside stars — all are the domain of statistical mechanics.

Deeper positioning:
- **The macro ↔ micro bridge**: it connects quantum mechanics and thermodynamics. Statistical mechanics lies between the Schrödinger equation and Carnot efficiency
- **The foundation of condensed matter physics**: superconductivity, superfluidity, magnetism, semiconductors — every macroscopic quantum phenomenon is analyzed in the language of statistical mechanics
- **A deep connection to field theory**: four-dimensional quantum field theory and four-dimensional statistical mechanics are equivalent under a Wick rotation ($t \to i\tau$) — a big topic
- **The universality of phase transitions**: the same critical exponents even when the microscopic detail differs → a unifying principle of nature (the renormalization group, Wilson)
- **Black hole entropy, AdS/CFT**: the meeting of gravity and statistical mechanics
- **A point of contact with machine learning**: Boltzmann machines, energy-based models — a direct application of statistical mechanics
- **Life, neuroscience, finance**: nonequilibrium statistical mechanics is a new frontier

Richard Feynman: "If all scientific knowledge were to be destroyed and only one sentence could be passed to the next generation, I would pass on 'everything is made of atoms.'" — statistical mechanics draws the entire macroscopic world out of this one sentence.

## Historical context
**The beginning of the kinetic theory of gases (1850s–1870s)**:
- Clausius, Maxwell: treating a gas as a collection of small particles and handling it statistically
- **Maxwell distribution (1860)**: the velocity distribution of a gas at equilibrium — the first quantitative result of statistical mechanics
- Boyle's and Charles's laws derived from microscopic laws

**Boltzmann's H-theorem (1872)**:
- An attempt to explain irreversibility (the increase of $S$) from microscopic collisions
- "The reversibility objection (Loschmidt)": if the microscopic laws are time-reversible, how does macroscopic irreversibility arise? — Boltzmann gives a statistical answer, but it was not accepted in his day
- In 1906 Boltzmann took his own life. On his tombstone: $S = k \log W$

**Twentieth-century consolidation**:
- Gibbs (1902, *Elementary Principles in Statistical Mechanics*): introduced the ensemble concept, completing the present formalism
- Einstein (1905): Brownian motion → proof of the reality of molecules
- Bose, Einstein (1924): photon statistics → the start of quantum statistics
- Fermi, Dirac (1926): electron statistics → the starting point of condensed matter and metal theory
- Onsager (1944): the exact solution of the 2D Ising model → the first result to go beyond mean-field theory
- Wilson (1971): the renormalization group → explaining the universality of critical phenomena, a Nobel Prize

**Twenty-first-century expansion**:
- Nonequilibrium statistical mechanics: the Jarzynski equality, the Crooks fluctuation theorem
- Fusion with quantum statistical mechanics and information theory
- The foundation of chaos and complex-systems theory

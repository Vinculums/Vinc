# Hamiltonian Mechanics

## One-line essence
The state of a physical system is a single point in the (position, momentum) phase space, and time evolution is that point flowing along the energy contour lines.

## Physical picture
Picture a pendulum. To describe any given moment, you need two things — **where it is** (the angle $q$) and **how it is moving** (the momentum $p$). Lagrange viewed this as $(q, \dot{q})$, but Hamilton views it as $(q, p)$. A subtle difference, but the consequences are enormous.

Now draw a plane with $q$ on the horizontal axis and $p$ on the vertical axis. This is **phase space**. The state of the pendulum at each instant is a single point on this plane. As time passes, the point moves — how?

The pendulum's energy $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kq^2$ is constant. That is, the point always stays on a contour line of the same $H$ value. The phase-space trajectory of a simple pendulum is an **ellipse**. With friction, it is drawn inward into a spiral. A chaotic system draws a bizarre attractor (strange attractor) in phase space.

The key insight: **all dynamics is a flow in phase space**. Like a liquid flowing, a swarm of state points flows in phase space. And this flow conserves volume (Liouville's theorem). This connects directly to statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics.

## Mathematical structure
**The Hamiltonian** (the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian):
$$H(q, p, t) = \sum_i p_i \dot{q}_i - L(q, \dot{q}, t)$$

In the usual case $H = T + V$ = total energy.

**Hamilton's canonical equations**:
$$\dot{q}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}$$

The meaning of each term:
- Lagrange's second-order differential equation → decomposed into two first-order simultaneous equations
- $q$ and $p$ are treated as **equal-standing variables** (previously $p$ was a by-product of $\dot{q}$)

**The Poisson bracket**: measures the "dynamical relationship" of two functions in phase space.
$$\{f, g\} = \sum_i \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right)$$

The time change of a physical quantity $f$:
$$\frac{df}{dt} = \{f, H\} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}$$

Therefore, if $\{f, H\} = 0$ then $f$ is a conserved quantity. Conservation laws simplify into an algebraic structure.

**Fundamental brackets**:
$$\{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}, \qquad \{q_i, q_j\} = \{p_i, p_j\} = 0$$

Going over to quantum mechanics, this becomes $[q, p] = i\hbar$ — the canonical commutation relation.

**Liouville's theorem**: the volume element of phase space is conserved under time evolution.
$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = 0$$
($\rho$: phase-space density). The starting point of statistical mechanics.

## Why it matters
Hamiltonian mechanics is **the bridge connecting the classical and the quantum**. The canonical quantization of quantum mechanics is essentially a one-line substitution, "Poisson bracket → commutator":
$$\{f, g\}_{\text{classical}} \to \frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{f}, \hat{g}]_{\text{quantum}}$$

This simple dictionary leads all the way to the Schrödinger equation, the Heisenberg equation, and quantum field theory.

Deeper positioning:
- **The foundation of statistical mechanics**: phase-space volume = number of microstates → entropy $S = k_B \ln \Omega$
- **The womb of quantum mechanics**: $H \to \hat{H}$, the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar \partial_t |\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle$
- **The stage of chaos theory**: phase-space geometry is itself the dynamics — the KAM theorem, attractors, Lyapunov exponents
- **Symplectic geometry**: phase space is not a mere plane but a **symplectic manifold**. One pillar of modern mathematical physics
- **The standard of numerical integration**: symplectic integrators are the standard technique for long-term celestial simulations (the solar system over 100 million years)

If the Lagrangian is "the language of field theory," the Hamiltonian is "the language of quantization." Both are essential.

## Historical context
**William Rowan Hamilton** (1805-1865) was an Irish genius. He started from optics. He discovered that "the path of a light ray is the variation of the optical action = 0" (Hamilton's principle), and carried this over directly into mechanics.

In his 1834 paper the canonical equations appeared. In decomposing Lagrange's second-order equations into first order, the beauty of the equal-standing pair of variables $(q,p)$ was revealed.

Background puzzles:
- Is the formal similarity between optics and mechanics ("light ray" $\leftrightarrow$ "particle") a coincidence, or essential?
- Is there no more elegant way to handle perturbation calculations in celestial mechanics?
- Hamilton's answer: **a unified formalism based on the variational principle** handles both simultaneously

Jacobi (1837) developed this further with the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, which a hundred years later became the decisive inspiration for Schrödinger when deriving the wave equation of quantum mechanics.

Poincaré (in the 1890s) understood phase space geometrically in celestial mechanics, sowing the seeds of chaos, and Dirac (1925) discovered the correspondence between the Poisson bracket and the commutator, completing the formalism of quantum mechanics.
